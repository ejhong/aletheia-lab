The ten gases of the 2017 paper

Does the rebuilt calculation give the reported split, with anaesthetics slowing the vibration and look-alike gases speeding it up?

Mostly. 7 of the 8 anaesthetics slow it and both look-alike gases speed it up. The paper's other result, that stronger drugs shift it more, did not come out (correlation +0.12). The rebuilt version uses a different structure, different positions for the gases and a different estimate of their properties, and its shifts are far larger than the paper's, so this is not a full reproduction.

Partly reproduced

Other proteins

Is the split special to the microtubule protein?

No. Egg-white lysozyme gives the same 7 of 8 and 2 of 2. So does a cloud of 81 rings placed at random. In 303 of 315 cases across nine targets, the direction of the shift is what the size of the gas molecule alone would give. In the paper's list, the two look-alike gases were the two largest molecules.

Same everywhere

More gases

Does the down-or-up split hold for 27 anaesthetics and 7 look-alikes?

No. 11 anaesthetics, among them the xylenes and methoxyflurane, speed the vibration up as the look-alikes did, and 2 small look-alikes slow it down. The size of the shift does not follow the strength of the drug (correlation −0.08). Anaesthetics and look-alikes overlap in size, so no rule based on size can sort them.

Does not hold

The paper's own measure

Counting only the largest slowing each gas causes, do anaesthetics outscore look-alikes?

Yes on tubulin: an anaesthetic outscores a look-alike 89% of the time, where 50% is chance, and size does not account for it. But a channel that anaesthetics are accepted to act on scores 85% and the blood protein albumin 83%. The small proteins and the random rings score lower (68% to 75%). The score does not rise with the strength of the drug (correlation −0.09). So the model does respond to where a gas sits in a real protein, but not to tubulin in particular.

The method as described

Does the answer depend on which way round the protein file happens to sit?

On our reading of the paper's description, yes: turning the whole structure changes the shift, and for one gas reverses it (−3703 to +2722 GHz). A second reading of the same sentence has no such problem, the authors' program is not public, and the formulas as printed do not give the paper's own frequency range. So this is a doubt about the description, not a demonstrated error in the published numbers. A version with the dependence removed sorts the gases better on one tubulin structure (79%) but at chance on the other (51%).
