Against the first argument

You would have to know that you are consistent.

Who makes it

Chalmers, 1995, §1; earlier replies of the kind came from Putnam in 1960 and Benacerraf in 1967. Gödel's theorem gives only "if F is consistent, its Gödel sentence is true", and no one can check that of the system that is themselves.

What the check does to it

Confirmed

The machine proves that same conditional. All it lacks is knowledge that it is consistent, and supplying that gives a second machine with the same gap.

Against the first argument

At most, you cannot know which machine you are.

Who makes it

Gödel, in conversation with Hao Wang; Benacerraf, 1967; made exact by Reinhardt, 1985. A mind can be a machine so long as it does not know which one, or does not know that the machine is reliable.

What the check does to it

Confirmed

Both halves are now checked theorems. "I know which machine I am" is refuted. "I am some machine" is not, and yields a truth I can never know.

Against the second argument

Knowing your own soundness is contradictory, machine or not.

Who makes it

Chalmers, 1995, §2. The contradiction Penrose finds comes from "I know that I am sound", so that assumption should go, not the assumption of being a machine.

What the check does to it

Confirmed, with a limit

Proved under the conditions Chalmers states, with no machine in the proof. But it needs sentences that speak of their own knowledge. Without them, knowing your own soundness is consistent.

Against the second argument

Two of its steps use rules where they do not apply.

Who makes it

Koellner, 2016 and 2018. Truth and knowledge are being applied to sentences that are neither true nor false. Restrict the rules to sentences that are one or the other, and the argument cannot be run.

What the check does to it

Half checked

Proved: the rules contradict themselves unrestricted, and the restricted argument needs two facts. Quoted, not proved: that those facts fail.

Against both

No one is an ideal reasoner.

Who makes it

Koellner, 2018, with Boolos and Shapiro before him. "What an ideal mathematician could know" may be too vague a notion for the question to have an answer.

What the check does to it

Not touched

Every statement here is about an ideal reasoner. A proof checker cannot say whether there is such a thing.
