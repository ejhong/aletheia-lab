## What was asked

On 11 February 2026 *PLOS One* retracted two papers from 2025 by researchers who hold that a comet struck at the start of the Younger Dryas, the cold spell that began about 12,900 years ago.

- **The Baffin Bay paper** (Moore and others) reported a layer of tiny metal-rich spheres, metallic dust, melted glass and platinum-rich particles in four cores of sea-floor mud between Canada and Greenland, dated to the start of the cold.
- **The shocked quartz paper** (Kennett and others) reported sixteen quartz grains with glass-filled fractures at three archaeological sites in California, New Mexico and Arizona, in the layer of that age and not in samples above or below it.

The journal's notices give reasons. For the first paper: the dates were calibrated wrongly and come out too old; five pictures of "spherule fragments" show marine microfossils; too little of the cores was sampled; and many references point to the wrong papers. For the second: not all the available dates were used, and the sections were not sampled through.

Eight authors of each paper did not agree. The lead author of the first told Retraction Watch that "the core issue is procedural" and that the group stands by its data, methods and conclusions.

Most of the data behind both papers is still public. The question fixed in advance was which of the journal's checkable reasons hold up against it.

## The Baffin Bay cores

### The dates

Shells from the sea floor look older to radiocarbon than they are, because sea water holds old carbon. Calibration corrects for this with a curve for marine samples and a local allowance, called ΔR, for water that is older still. The paper says which it used: the marine curve, and allowances of +71, +218 and +103 years. A positive allowance makes an age younger.

The ages printed in the paper's table of dates are not the ones that method gives.

- For **three cores** (64, 67 and 77), 16 of the 17 printed ages are reproduced, to within three years, by the marine curve with the allowance entered as −218 and −103: the stated values with the sign reversed. That makes each age older instead of younger.
- For the **fourth core** (52), five of the six new dates are reproduced to within a year by the curve for land samples, which leaves out the sea's old carbon altogether. The date on the claimed layer itself does not fit that or any other recipe, unless its radiocarbon age was 11,020 and not the 11,120 printed.

No other curve or allowance we tried reproduces more than two dates in any core. We can say which calculation reproduces the table. We cannot say how it came about, and the plots in the paper's own supplement are labelled with the marine curve throughout.

The figure shows the four dates on the claimed layer, as printed and as the stated method gives them. The buttons add the larger allowances that polar seas are thought to have needed during the ice age.

{{figure: yd-retracted-plos-audit/baffin_14c_sensitivity}}

With the stated method and nothing added, the four dates are 297 to 608 years younger than printed. Two of the cores can no longer be at the start of the cold at all, and the other two have between one chance in six and one in four. With a further 500 years for polar water, the test fixed in advance, none of the four can. The curve's own authors recommend, for seas this far north, treating the result as a bracket between the ordinary allowance and a much larger one; at the young end of that bracket all four layers are later than the whole Younger Dryas, which is what the journal's notice says.

### The pictures of "spherule fragments"

The supplement shows five figures captioned as spherule fragments from one core. They show two objects. Both are calcium carbonate, 92.5 to 96.5% calcium, carbon and oxygen. One has a wall pierced by regular pores and is plainly the shell of a foraminifer, a marine microfossil; the other is less distinctive. The journal's reading holds.

Two details matter for what follows from it. The sample they come from is about 9,500 years old by the paper's own table, more than 3,000 years after the layer in question, though the paper also draws its pictures of melted glass from it. And that sample is not among the counted ones, so these particular grains did not add to the counts. How many of the grains counted by eye elsewhere were microfossils cannot be known from what is published.

### How much was sampled

The counted samples add up to 85 cm of 27.6 metres of core, about 3%, nearly all of it close to the claimed layer. The notice points to a sample in one core, outside the layer, as rich as the layer in another. That is right for metallic dust (680 particles per kilogram against 640) and not for spheres (none in that sample). In one core the spheres peak in the sample above the claimed layer; in another the dated sample has none.

### The chemistry

The paper measured 18 spheres. As measured, none has the iron, nickel and chromium of comet dust, and the paper says so. It then supposes each is 1% cosmic material, multiplies the nickel and chromium by 100, and finds the result overlaps comet dust.

That step cannot tell a sphere with cosmic material from one without. The chart applies it to ordinary crust.

{{figure: yd-retracted-plos-audit/baffin_x100_math}}

Average continental crust, with nothing cosmic in it, lands in the same place. The measurements also go against the supposition: a sphere whose nickel was mostly cosmic would have more nickel for its iron than crust has, and these have less. The authors present the step as an assumption and claim only that the result is "consistent with" 1% cosmic material. The journal's notice does not mention it. This is our own point.

Twelve measures of the platinum-rich particles were tested for a peak at the claimed layer. In each core one of the twelve peaks there, and chance would give two.

### The references

The notice says DOIs in the reference list lead to the wrong papers. Looked up one by one, 24 of 159 fail: 17 do not exist and 7 belong to something else. One reference to a paper on Antarctic sediment carries the DOI of an article on the history of Buddhism in India.

## The shocked quartz

{{figure: yd-retracted-plos-audit/quartz_audit}}

**The dating.** The paper says three radiocarbon dates, one per site, pass a test of agreement. The three numbers it tested are not dates. They are the outputs of statistical models from a 2015 paper, two of them with errors of 235 and 365 years. With errors that large the test could hardly fail: one site would have to be more than 500 years from the others before it did. The paper's own tables show 2 of the 12 dates from the lower part of the Arlington Canyon section, and those twelve are not one radiocarbon age. But five of the twelve are on wood or carbon that the original excavators judged older than the layer, and with those set aside the disagreement is modest. At the other two sites the published dates are on Clovis camps and not on the sampled layer.

So the journal's point holds in this form: the paper does not show that the three layers are the same age. It also does not show they differ.

**The sampling.** Of 66 samples listed at the three sites, 13 were examined for shocked quartz: three in the layer, with 5, 7 and 4 grains, and ten above or below it, with none. The paper says how many quartz grains were searched in each layer sample (3,000 to 18,000) and for none of the ten others, so "none found" has no measure of how hard anyone looked. At Arlington Canyon the three comparison samples lie inside the layer as the authors themselves define it. The journal's point holds.

**What was not tested.** Whether the sixteen grains are shocked quartz, and what made them. The journal did not question the microscopy and we could not examine it.

**A word on the label.** The label fixed in advance for this paper was "retraction mixed or harsh". It could not have come out stronger, since the rule required the journal to have faulted the microscopy and it was already known that it had not. The first write-up went on to call the retraction harsher than standard practice. It cited no standard, and the guidelines the journal follows allow retraction when conclusions are unreliable, which is what the notice says. We withdraw that statement. Whether the journal judged rightly is a matter on which the authors disagree, and the data cannot settle it.

## Spot check: what holds and what does not

What holds:

- The plan is unchanged since it was locked, and the program gives the same numbers when rerun.
- Every table typed in by hand from an image matches the image.
- The recalibrated dates, checked with a separately written program, agree to within two years.
- The label for the Baffin Bay paper: four checks of five came out against it, where three were needed.

What did not:

- **A list of dates was partly invented.** The first run's "25 published dates" for the quartz sites did not match the tables it cited: four laboratory numbers do not exist there and others carried the wrong ages. Its headline statistic for the quartz paper rested on that list and is withdrawn. The list has been rebuilt from the source.
- **"Cherry-picked" was wrong.** The first write-up said the authors chose a date from above the quartz sample for their test. The test did not use that date.
- **Words about what the authors did.** "Flipped the sign", "accidentally", "bug" and "trick" claimed to know how errors arose or what was intended. They are gone.
- **The dating finding was off the record.** The reversed sign was found after the plan was locked, by a search that was in no committed program. It is now reproduced by the spot check's own program and labelled as found after the fact. It holds for 16 of 17 dates, not all 17, and is weaker for the fourth core than the first write-up said.
- **Four checks had been altered after the lock without a record.** They are back in their locked form. No label changed.
- **Smaller numbers:** "16.7% of the cores sampled" was a span, and the samples are 3%; an "arithmetic error" in one table is a misprinted weight; pores described as 1 to 2 micrometres are about half a micrometre.
- **"Four of five checks" is not "four of five of the journal's reasons".** Two of the five test arguments the journal never made.

## What this means

For the Baffin Bay paper the journal's reasons hold, and the dating is worse than the notice says: with the method the paper itself describes, the layers are a few hundred to more than a thousand years too young to mark the start of the cold. For the shocked quartz paper the journal's two reasons mostly hold: the claim that three sites share one date rests on a test that could not have failed, and too little of each section was examined to show the grains are special to one layer. Neither result shows that nothing fell. Sixteen grains with glass-filled fractures were found where the authors said, and nobody has shown anything wrong with them. They need what they have not had: other laboratories looking at the same slides, and at slides from above and below, without knowing which is which.
