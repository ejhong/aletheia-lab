> **Where things stand, 23 November 2026.** Finished and replicated. Across Greenland as a whole, 1908 is an ordinary year for ammonium. Site by site it is not: a sharp spike at two of six sites and at the summit, nothing in the north-west. Whether Tunguska or the forest fires of that summer made it, the ice cannot say.

## What we figured out

On the morning of 30 June 1908 something exploded several kilometres above the Tunguska river in Siberia and flattened about two thousand square kilometres of forest. It left no crater. It is the one large airburst of which we are certain.

One form of the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis says the Earth met many such airbursts, year after year, from the debris of a broken comet. Earlier studies here found a century of high ammonium in Greenland ice after the Younger Dryas cold began. To judge whether airbursts could have put it there, it helps to know what a single airburst leaves. A published paper says Tunguska left an ammonium spike in ice from Greenland's summit. We asked whether the rest of Greenland shows it.

{{figure: tunguska-calibration/tiles}}

1. **Across Greenland as a whole, no.** Averaged over six sites away from the summit, 1908 and 1909 rank 10th of 56 pairs of years. By the rule set in advance that is "ordinary".
2. **But 1908 is not empty.** At NEGIS in the north-east, ammonium in 1908 was seven times a typical year. At ACT11d in the south-west it was three and a half times, by that core's dates in 1909. Two more cores from the summit, which were kept out of the test, confirm the spike that prompted the question: five and six times a typical year.
3. **And two sites hold nothing.** At NEEM and Humboldt in the north-west, 1908 is an unremarkable year. At NEEM it is the lowest of its decade.
4. **That is how smoke arrives.** A plume crosses some of the ice sheet and misses the rest. Where the spike falls it is sharp, weeks to months long, and at the summit it comes with soot.
5. **The ice cannot say what burned.** Tunguska was on 30 June. The northern fire season runs from July to September. The spikes sit in the second half of 1908, which fits both.

## The test

{{figure: tunguska-calibration/ranks}}

Each bar is two years, and its height says how far ammonium stood above the thirty years around it, averaged over the six sites. A mark spread through the Arctic air would lift every site and push 1908 to the top. It is in the upper part of the pack and no more.

The test could have seen a general mark. With a made-up pulse of two typical years' ammonium added to every site, it was caught nine times in ten. What 1908 left is a different thing: far more than that at a few sites, nothing at others.

## A closer look at the spikes

This part was looked at after the result and is exploratory.

- **They are sharp.** The largest single sample at NEGIS is 32 times a typical one. In the two summit cores it is 50 and 15 times.
- **There is soot with them at the summit**, three to four times the usual, and about double the usual nitrate.
- **They fall in the second half of 1908.** Dates inside a year are rough, but each core carries a better clock: the sulphur from the eruption of Katmai in June 1912. The ammonium spike sits 4.2 years before that sulphur in both summit cores, 4.3 at NGRIP and 3.9 at ACT11d. That places them all in the same months, including the one ACT11d's own dates put in 1909.
- **It was 1908 and not 1910.** The first record we saw could not separate 1908 from the great North American fires of 1910. These can.
- **Something else happened in 1906.** Two cores at Tunu, set aside before the test because their dating is loose, hold an even larger spike dated 1906 that two better-dated cores at the same place do not show. We have not explained it.

## What it means for the Younger Dryas

In the century and a half after the Younger Dryas cold began, about 2.3 times as much ammonium reached the ice each year as before. Could a rain of Tunguskas have done that?

Take the two possibilities in turn.

**If the 1908 spike was Tunguska's**, then an airburst leaves a sharp, sooty spike at some sites and nothing at others. A century of them would fill the ice with spikes. The ice of that century has [fewer spikes than usual](../ice-core-fire/) and a slow, steady rise.

**If it was that summer's fires**, then Tunguska itself left nothing these records can see, and airbursts of its size are not a source of ammonium worth counting.

Neither reading helps the idea. In plain totals, too, the numbers are against it: if Tunguska left a general mark at all it was probably under two typical years' worth, and matching the Younger Dryas rise would then take more than about two such events every three years for 150 years.

This is one event, in Siberia, far from Greenland and not upwind of it. Objects of a different kind, or very many small ones, need not behave like it.

## How it was done

- **Public data.** Thirteen ice cores from nine Greenland sites, measured several times a year, from three laboratories.
- **Found blind.** The agent that located and downloaded the files was told not to open any chemistry from 1895 to 1920, and the program was built with 1905 to 1912 removed.
- **Dating checked first.** Each core's sulphur was compared with a core tied to historical eruptions. Four cores failed and were set aside before the test.
- **Reviewed, then locked.** Google's model criticised the plan. Its review rebuilt the test: windows that do not overlap, the same sites in every window, no tinkering with dates, and no claim to tell an airburst from a fire.
- **Replicated.** Google's model wrote its own program from the plan alone. Every rank, score and verdict matched.
- **Cost.** $1.42.

## What went wrong

- **The first plan had three real flaws**, all found by the outside review before anything was run.
- **The test answers a narrower question than the interesting one.** It asks about Greenland as a whole. The finding is the pattern from site to site, which the plan reports and does not test.
- **Some choices were made knowing the other years.** The window, the thresholds and the choice of cores were fixed after seeing every year but 1905 to 1912.

## What this cannot tell you

- Whether Tunguska or forest fires made the 1908 spike.
- What a different kind of object would leave, or thousands of small ones.
- Anything exact about the Younger Dryas. The comparison is an illustration of scale.
- Four of the six test sites were measured in one laboratory and dated in one family of chronologies, so they are not independent witnesses.
- One AI session proposed, designed, ran and wrote up the study. No human has checked the numbers.
