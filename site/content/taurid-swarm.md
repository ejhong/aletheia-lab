> **Where things stand, 23 November 2026.** Finished. Run by Antigravity (Google DeepMind); its headline numbers were recomputed from fresh downloads by Claude (Anthropic) and matched. None of four tests set in advance found a swarm of large fragments. The lining-up of large asteroids that first suggested one is still there, in the same seventeen bodies, and goes no further.

## What we figured out {menu: Findings}

Every autumn the Earth passes through the Taurid meteor stream, the dust of a small comet called Encke. In the 1980s the astronomers Victor Clube and Bill Napier proposed that Encke and the Taurids are what is left of a giant comet, fifty to a hundred kilometres across, that came into the inner solar system twenty or thirty thousand years ago and broke up. On their account the stream still carries a swarm of large fragments, held together by the pull of Jupiter, and the Earth meets it now and then. This is the form of the Younger Dryas impact idea that does without a single day of catastrophe.

In 2010 Napier pointed to evidence in the sky. Among the largest near-Earth asteroids on orbits like Encke's, most have their closest point to the Sun in the same direction as Encke's. Unrelated asteroids should point every which way.

NASA's catalogue now holds far more near-Earth asteroids than it did then, and its list of airbursts seen by United States government satellites runs to thirty-two full years. If there is a swarm of large fragments, both should show it. Four tests were fixed before either was looked at.

{{figure: taurid-swarm/verdict}}

1. **The lining-up is still there among the largest, and it is not new evidence.** Of the 17 largest asteroids on Taurid-like orbits, 11 point within 45 degrees of Encke, where chance would give about 4. But these are the bodies Napier counted. Large asteroids were nearly all known by 2010, so the catalogue has not tested his observation again; it has repeated it.
2. **It does not continue to smaller bodies.** A comet that breaks up makes many more small pieces than large ones. Down to about a kilometre across there are 64 asteroids on such orbits, and 31% point Encke's way, against 25% by chance. Down to about 400 metres there are 331, and 22% do. Of the 47 added between the first two steps, 9 line up. The newer, smaller asteroids show no preference at all.
3. **Few bodies sit where the swarm should be.** The swarm is supposed to be trapped on orbits that circle the Sun seven times for every two of Jupiter's. Down to about 400 metres, 13 aligned asteroids are in that band, which is not clearly more than the comparison groups give. Of the three largest, two are bright and stony, not dark like a comet.
4. **Satellites saw no extra airbursts.** In the autumn Taurid weeks over 32 years there were 133, where an even spread through the year gives 134.5. In the summer weeks when the stream arrives from the Sun's direction there were 81 against 88.7. In the six years when the swarm was due back, the share of airbursts falling in the Taurid weeks was 12.7%, against 13.0% in other years.

## Which way the orbits point {menu: Directions}

{{figure: taurid-swarm/radar}}

| Asteroids on Taurid-like orbits | How many | Share pointing within 45° of Encke (chance: 25%) |
|---|---|---|
| The largest, about 2 km and up | 17 | 65% |
| Down to about 1 km | 64 | 31% |
| Down to about 400 m | 331 | 22% |
| Down to about 150 m | 848 | 21% |
| All catalogued | 2,470 | 20% |

One comparison group behaved in a way worth knowing. Asteroids on rounder orbits of the same size point strongly *away* from Encke, because Jupiter herds them toward its own direction. Against that group the Taurid-like orbits look aligned at every size. Against asteroids on equally stretched orbits at steeper tilts, which Jupiter does not herd, they do not. The second is the fairer comparison, and it shows nothing below the largest seventeen.

## Airbursts seen by satellite {menu: Airbursts}

Cameras on the ground do record more bright Taurid meteors in the years the swarm is due back, from pieces the size of pebbles to footballs (as the study reports from published work; not checked here). The question is whether that extends to bodies metres across, which explode high in the air and are picked up by satellites built to watch for other things.

{{figure: taurid-swarm/fireballs}}

It does not, as far as 1,045 airbursts can show.

## What does not fit so neatly

- **A hint among the smallest.** Down to about 150 metres, 36 of 174 aligned asteroids sit in the seven-to-two band, against 12% of the unaligned ones: about 15 more than expected, and unlikely by chance against that group (about 1 in 400). Against the other comparison group it is borderline. This size was a side check and not one of the four tests, and the catalogue is far from complete so small. It is the one place where something like the predicted swarm shows.
- **The largest seventeen.** Their lining-up is real and unexplained by this study. It is a small number of bodies, several of them stony, and the same data cannot both suggest and confirm a pattern.

## How it was done

- **Two public NASA archives:** the catalogue of 43,156 near-Earth asteroids with their orbits and brightness, and the list of airbursts recorded by United States government satellites, 1,045 of them in the 32 full years from 1994 to 2025.
- **Locked first.** The orbit box, the sizes, the weeks, the swarm years and the pass marks were fixed before any Taurid number was computed.
- **Comparison groups,** so that Jupiter's herding of orbits and the habits of asteroid surveys could not pass for a swarm.
- **Checked by a second AI.** Claude downloaded both archives afresh and recomputed the headline numbers with its own short program: the counts at every size, the alignment figures and the airburst counts all came out the same. This was a spot check and not a full re-run.
- **Cost.** Nothing.

## What this cannot tell you

- **What the stream held 12,900 years ago.** A swarm of large pieces could have broken down or dispersed since. The tests are of large fragments today.
- **Small debris.** It is certainly there: it makes the Taurid meteors. Asteroid catalogues are badly incomplete below about a hundred metres. The study cites searches by other teams that put limits on such bodies in the swarm; we have not read them.
- **Whether a giant comet broke up.** Encke and the Taurids may well share a parent. The question here was narrower: whether a swarm of large fragments shows now.
- **Whether earlier crossings left marks on the Earth.** That is a separate question, [#104](https://github.com/ejhong/aletheia-lab/issues/104).
- AI systems designed, ran and checked this study. No astronomer has reviewed it.
