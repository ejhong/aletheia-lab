> **Where things stand, 23 November 2026.** Finished. The ice does hold something unusual: for about a century, ammonium ran higher than at almost any other time in 100,000 years, and three ice cores agree. But it starts thirty to forty years after the cold began, not with it; it is a century-long swell, not a sharp spike; and it drops back by half while dust stays high. It undercuts the idea that Greenland ammonium records an impact firestorm at the onset, without settling whether an impact happened for other reasons.

## What we figured out

Snow that falls on Greenland traps a little of what is in the air, and stays in order, year upon year. One thing it traps is ammonium, which northern wildfires, soils and plants give off. A great fire leaves a sharp spike.

The Younger Dryas was a sudden return to glacial cold near the end of the last ice age. One explanation—the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis (YDIH)—proposes that a comet or airburst struck the Earth and set whole regions alight, triggering an "impact winter". If so, the ice from that moment should carry a prompt ammonium spike unlike any other.

We took the 300 years around the start of the cold in three ice cores and compared them with every other 300-year stretch back to about 100,000 years ago. What counted as "exceptional" was written down and locked before any value from those 300 years had been looked at.

{{figure: ice-core-fire/verdict}}

Six things came out.

1. **The ice does stand out, partly.** Measured over 20 or 60 years, the ammonium high in that stretch ranks third to seventh among 131 to 323 stretches, in all three cores. That is the top 1 to 3 in a hundred. By the rule set in advance this is "unusual". It falls short of "exceptional", which needed the top one in a hundred in two cores.
2. **In plain concentration it is near the very top.** The 60 years from 12,810 to 12,750 years ago hold more ammonium than any other 60-year stretch we measured in the NGRIP core between 10,300 and 107,620 years ago. The other two cores rank it second and third.
3. **It does not begin when the cold begins.** At that moment (12,896 years before 2000), and for some decades after, ammonium is low. The rise starts thirty to forty years later and peaks seventy to a hundred and forty years later. Inside the GISP2 core itself, the big ammonium peak lies 2 to 3.5 metres above the depth cited for platinum.
4. **It is a century-long swell, not a sharp spike.** Year by year, nothing in it is exceptional: the median year jumps more than threefold, while the largest single year in the window comes *before* the cold and ranks tenth of 31.
5. **It is not alone, and thinner snow inflates it.** The last few thousand years of the ice age hold several highs of similar size at other rapid climate shifts—one near 13,870 years ago is larger in two of the three cores. Scaling NGRIP's excess by annual layer thickness (exploratory) drops the Younger Dryas window from second to fifth among just the 16 late-glacial windows.
6. **This undercuts impact fires, not the entire impact hypothesis.** The pattern fits a century-long climate-driven response—stressed northern forests dying, burning and releasing soil nitrogen as the cold sets in, amplified by thinner snow and drier air—far better than a day-one firestorm. On its own, however, Greenland ammonium cannot rule out an impact that did not light an instant North American firestorm (such as an impact on ice or ocean), nor does it test platinum or terrestrial impact proxies.

**Followed up.** A later question asked whether the other sudden coolings of the ice age leave the same mark. Mostly they do, in a smaller way: [ammonium commonly rises after a cooling](../cooling-ammonium/), and the Younger Dryas is among the largest cases, with two others close behind.

## What the ice shows

{{figure: ice-core-fire/profile}}

The vertical line marks the start of the cold, known to within a few years in NGRIP and NEEM. Look just to its left and right: these are some of the lowest bars in the picture. The tall bars come decades later.

A continent set alight by an impact—or ammonia made directly in a blast—should mark the ice in the same year or the next. A broad swell that waits a generation, builds for decades, and lasts a century is a different kind of event.

### Inside one core: depth order in GISP2

Because GISP2 uses its own timescale, it cannot be lined up to the decade with NGRIP and NEEM. Inside GISP2 itself, however, every chemical comes from the exact same ice samples, so the order in depth needs no timescale at all.

{{figure: ice-core-fire/gisp2}}

At 1712.2–1712.3 m—the depth supporters are reported to cite for platinum and fire (`unverified`; the supporters' paper and the body of Petaev et al. 2013 were not read)—ammonium is a modest 31.4 ppb, bordered immediately above and below by 9 to 15 ppb samples. The century-long ammonium high that drives GISP2's top rankings sits **2 to 3.5 metres higher in the core** (1710.7 to 1708.0 m), peaking at 77.3 ppb at 1708.8–1708.9 m—roughly 40 to 120 years *younger* than the 1712.2 m layer.

## How unusual

{{figure: ice-core-fire/ranks}}

Each row is one ice core, measured one way. The dot is the stretch at the start of the Younger Dryas; the ticks are all the other stretches.

Averaged over 20 or 60 years, the dot sits far to the right in every core: few stretches beat it. Averaged over 10 years it slips back just short of the top 5%, and at 3-year and 1-year resolution it is ordinary. A single great fire season would do the opposite: sharpest at fine scale, washed out at coarse scale. This pattern says "a lot of ammonium for a long time".

How close was it to "exceptional"? Close. In GISP2 at 60 years it missed the top 1% by a single place (4th where 3rd was needed). And when the local background is drawn using the higher of the medians before and after each bin—a pre-specified check for climate steps—NGRIP (1st) and NEEM (3rd) cross the 1% line at the 60-year scale. The verdict stays "unusual" because the rule was fixed first, but it is an "unusual" near the top of its range.

## The neighbours, and the effect of snowfall

{{figure: ice-core-fire/long}}

The end of the ice age was a busy time for ammonium. Once the first great warming arrives around 14,700 years ago, northern vegetation expands as the ice sheets retreat, the overall ammonium level rises, and big decadal highs become common. Of the ten highest stretches in the 20-year comparison, between five and eight in each core come from these 4,700 years—an eighth of the stretches compared.

Notice *where* the other big late-glacial highs sit: near **13,870 years ago** (largest in NEEM and GISP2, fifth in NGRIP—just after the Older Dryas cooling), near **13,050–13,130** (second in NEEM—around the Intra-Allerød Cold Period), and near **11,755** (second in GISP2—at the abrupt end of the Younger Dryas). Highs of this size turn up repeatedly around late-glacial climate shifts without a comet each time.

### Concentration is not fallout (exploratory)

One known limit of the locked test is that it measures **concentration** (µg per kg of ice), not **deposition per year**. When the Younger Dryas began, less snow fell on Greenland: NGRIP's annual layers thin from 4.01 cm/yr before the onset (12,900–13,050) to 3.01 cm/yr after it (12,750–12,890), and down to 2.75 cm/yr at the 3-year peak (12,774). The same fallout landing in a third less snow raises its concentration by a third to a half.

{{figure: ice-core-fire/flux}}

Multiplying each late-glacial NGRIP window's excess by its local layer thickness (`explore/after.json`) drops the Younger Dryas window from **2nd to 5th out of 16**. On top of thinner snow at Greenland, a shift to a cold, dry stadial also reduces rainout (wet scavenging) along the 6-to-10-day transport path from North America to Greenland (Schüpbach et al. 2018, *Nat. Commun.* 9:1476, read), allowing a larger share of emitted ammonium to survive the trip.

## Which explanation fits?

This section was added after the locked result and is **exploratory**: reasoning about what was found, plus a look at dust (calcium) and volcanic sulphate in the same ice (`explore/volcanic.py`).

Two questions are easy to tangle together here, and are clearer apart: **what put the ammonium in the ice?** and **what set off the Younger Dryas cold?**

### 1. What best explains the ammonium

The sharpest clue in the ice is not just that ammonium rises after the onset—it is **what happens after the first century**.

{{figure: ice-core-fire/dust}}

The Younger Dryas starts at 12,896 years ago with an abrupt 1-to-3-year shift in where Greenland's moisture came from (deuterium excess; Rasmussen et al. 2014, read). Cooling and windblown dust then ramp up over the next century. Watch the two panels together:

- **During the first century (12,850–12,750):** ammonium and dust climb together in all three cores.
- **After 12,750:** dust climbs *even higher* and stays high (140–315 µg/kg) while snow stays thin—**yet ammonium drops back by roughly half** (to about 17–23 µg/kg, still roughly double its pre-onset level of 8–11 µg/kg).

That two-stage shape rules out *both* extremes. If the ammonium high were only thinner snow and drier winds, it would stay at 40+ µg/kg alongside the dust. If it were a day-one impact firestorm, it would spike at 12,896 and end within a year or two.

What fits all five facts—the 30–40 year delay, the century-long duration, the drop back while dust stays high, the flux scaling, and the neighbouring highs at other rapid shifts—is a **two-part climate-transition response**:

1. **A ~100-year terrestrial overshoot (~2× above the new cold baseline).** During the warm Allerød, boreal forest and nitrogen-rich soils had spread far north behind the retreating ice sheet. When abrupt cooling and drying hit at 12,896, forests did not die and burn on day one; thermal stress, dieback, increased burning of dying woodland, and disturbed soils releasing nitrogen play out over **decades to a century**. Once the Allerød forest retreated and the landscape settled into a cold-adapted tundra/open-woodland state, that transient pulse ended and ammonium fell back. The [follow-up comparison of 25 sudden coolings](../cooling-ammonium/) reinforces this: earlier glacial coolings show an immediate step-up (shared with sea salt and nitrate), while the Younger Dryas—the only one hitting an expanding late-glacial forest—adds a distinctive second climb 60 to 160 years in.
2. **A permanent stadial step (~1.5×–2× from thinner snow and drier air).** Lower snowfall on Greenland plus reduced wet scavenging along the transport path from North America (Schüpbach et al. 2018) amplifies concentrations throughout the cold period—turning a ~2× terrestrial pulse into a 3–4× concentration peak during the transition, and keeping later Younger Dryas ammonium near ~20 µg/kg even after the overshoot ends.

**What remains open: fire versus soil ammonia.** Ammonium in Greenland ice comes from both boreal wildfires and background soil/plant emissions (Fischer et al. 2015, read). In annual NGRIP data (`explore/`), the *median* year jumps 3.6-fold during the high (7.0 to 25.5 µg/kg, with 47 of 108 years above 30 µg/kg) while annual spike scores (`Z`) stay ordinary. That points toward chronically elevated background (soil/biogenic emissions plus dry-air/thin-snow amplification) and/or frequent moderate burning across decades, rather than a few mega-fire seasons. Separating those two requires decomposing the 1 mm seasonal baseline from individual fire plumes (as in Fischer et al. 2015), or checking co-located combustion-only tracers such as black carbon, levoglucosan and formate.

### 2. How each trigger hypothesis fits the ammonium

| Explanation | What it predicts for ammonium | Fit with the ice |
|---|---|---|
| **One impact that lit fires at the onset** | A sharp spike at the onset | **Poor.** Ammonium is low at the onset, waits 30–40 years, and sits 2–3.5 m above the cited platinum depth in GISP2. |
| **An airburst at the onset (fires, or ammonia made in the blast)** | A prompt pulse, within a few years | **Poor**, unless the pulse was small enough to hide in the noise (see below). |
| **Airbursts spread over a century** | Raised ammonium for a century | **Not excluded; not called for.** Would have to wait 30–40 years, track dust, stop when dust plateaus, and mimic other late-glacial transitions. |
| **Ocean circulation slowed (AMOC)** | Nothing at the trigger; a climate response afterwards | **Good.** Untested here; matches any cooling trigger that is not itself a source of ammonium. |
| **Volcanic eruption(s) set off the cold** | Nothing at the trigger; a climate response afterwards | **Good for ammonium**; GISP2 sulphate gives weak support at best (below). |
| **An impact on ice or ocean (no onset firestorm)** | Nothing at the trigger; a climate response afterwards | **Not ruled out by ammonium**—but removes ammonium as evidence *for* an impact. |

- **Airbursts.** In the airburst form of the YDIH, fires are lit from above, or ammonia is made directly in the shock front (Melott et al. 2010, *Geology* 38:355–358; abstract read on arXiv:0907.1067). Either way, ammonium should arrive promptly: large volcanic eruptions in this same ice, which do load the whole stratosphere, appear as sulphate spikes a single sample wide (four to five years), not decades late. Nitrate—which Melott et al. also expect from impact shock—is high in NGRIP (5th of 320), ordinary in NEEM (198th of 320), and in between in GISP2, so the cores do not agree on it.
- **Volcanism.** Volcanoes emit sulphate, not ammonium, so they predict no ammonium at the trigger and a climate response afterwards. In GISP2's same samples (shown in the depth diagram above), two large sulphate spikes precede the dust rise by about 50 and 150 years (462 ppb at 1713.8 m and 649 ppb at 1718.6 m). That is the right order, but weak support: spikes that size happen roughly once in 800 years here (~6% chance of falling within 50 years before any given date by coincidence), and by the same window test sulphate around the onset is ordinary (36th of 132 at 20 years, 211th of 323 at 60). The eruption often linked to the Younger Dryas, Laacher See, is dated by Reinig et al. 2021 (*Nature* 595:66–69; search summary, `unverified`) to about 200 years before the onset, though that date has been disputed.
- **Ocean circulation.** The standard paleoclimate account—freshwater or Arctic sea-ice discharge slowing the Atlantic overturning circulation—predicts the same ammonium pattern as volcanism, and nothing in this study separates the two.

**Bottom line on the YDIH:** This single analysis does **not** by itself rule out a cosmic impact at the Younger Dryas—it tests Greenland ammonium only, not GISP2 platinum, terrestrial microspherules, meltglass, or records outside Greenland's airshed. What it *does* do is remove one of the YDIH's flagship arguments: Greenland ammonium does not look like an impact-ignited continental firestorm or "impact winter" at the onset, and behaves instead like a century-long ecological and atmospheric response to abrupt cooling.

## Could the test have missed a sharp spike?

To check what size of single-year fire the test could see, we added made-up one-year pulses of ammonium at 300 random places in each real record and scored them with the same rule.

| Size of one-year burst (µg/kg·yr) | Rough scale (ordinary = 6 µg/kg) | Share caught at top 1% |
|---|---|---|
| 200 | ~35 years' worth in one year | Almost never (0–2%) |
| 500 | ~85 years' worth | Mixed: 1% to 92%, depending on core and bin |
| 1,000 | ~170 years' worth | Nearly always in 6 of 8 series (63–100%) |
| 2,000 | ~340 years' worth | Nearly always (93–100%) |

So "no sharp spike" means no single-year burst of several hundred units or more—a smaller prompt fire could hide in the noise. For comparison, the century-long swell actually found adds up to roughly 300 to 800 µg/kg·yr spread across decades.

## How it was done

- **Public data.** Ammonium measured along the NGRIP and NEEM cores by the University of Bern group (Erhardt et al. 2021, released 2021, CC-BY-4.0), and along the GISP2 core by the Glacier Research Group (Mayewski et al. 1997, NOAA). Two laboratories, two measuring methods.
- **Like with like.** Old ice is squeezed thin, so a spike looks smaller the older it is. Each tier uses one averaging length and one time span across all its cores, chosen so the oldest ice still resolves it.
- **Locked first.** The plan was criticised twice by two other vendors' models and rebuilt before it was locked. Nothing from 12,000–14,000 years ago was looked at until after the lock.
- **Replicated twice.** OpenAI's and Google's models each wrote an independent program from the written plan alone. Both reproduced every window value, every rank and the verdict.
- **Cost.** $2.74 of the $25 budget.

## What went wrong, and what this cannot tell you

- **We could not read the supporters' paper.** Wolbach et al. 2018 (*J. Geol.* 126:165–184) and Holliday et al. 2023 (*Earth-Sci. Rev.* 247:104502) are closed-access and copies online refused automated access; their claims and the exact GISP2 platinum/ammonium depths are quoted from search snippets and marked `unverified`.
- **The first plan was flawed.** It compared GISP2 at 60 years with NGRIP and NEEM at 10, and would have let two cores "agree" on peaks centuries apart. Two rounds of pre-lock review caught both.
- **One assumption in the plan turned out wrong.** Raw peak height (`H`) was set aside on the assumption that warm periods always have higher ammonium; here the high sits in a cold period. Raw peak heights are reported in full above.
- **Concentration, not full flux.** The locked test does not correct for snowfall; the NGRIP layer-thickness check above is exploratory, and no full accumulation-plus-transport model was run across all three cores.
- **Only one kind of comparison in this study.** We compared the onset against every 300-year stretch, not against other sudden coolings only. That climate-matched comparison has since been run in [a follow-up question](../cooling-ammonium/).
- **Narrow scope.** Greenland ammonium mostly sees North America (Fischer et al. 2015; Schüpbach et al. 2018). This study does not test platinum, microspherules, meltglass, nanodiamonds, or fires outside Greenland's airshed.
- **No human has checked the numbers.** AI models designed, ran, replicated and wrote up the study.
