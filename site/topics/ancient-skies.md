## The idea

The Earth's axis wobbles like a slowing top, once in about 26,000 years. As it does, the pole of the sky moves among the stars, the constellations that mark the seasons change, and the part of the southern sky that never rises shifts. All of this can be calculated exactly, forwards or back.

That makes the sky a clock. A star map with an empty patch where no stars rose, a carving said to show the constellations of a certain season, a temple pointed at a rising star: each can be asked what date it fits. Writers on lost civilisations have often claimed that these clocks read 10,500 BCE or thereabouts, far earlier than any astronomy known to history.

The claims are testable because the arithmetic is not in doubt. What is in doubt is how many ways there were to get a match. Each study here fixes the test first and counts the ways.

## What the studies add up to

One clock has been read so far, and it reads the wrong thing for the purpose.

The empty patch in the classical constellations does carry a date, and the date is that of the Greek astronomers who compiled the star catalogue, between about 130 BCE and 140 CE. That is the right answer to the question the record can answer. A cataloguer lists every star he can see, so the edge of the listed stars is his own horizon. The figures those stars belong to may be much older, and the catalogue is silent on it.

The study also showed how soft such dates are. The model named in advance as the main one put the catalogue in 910 CE, centuries after it was written, because of one assumption about haze near the horizon. Earlier published dates for the same patch, including one near 2600 BCE, were made with fewer checks than this, and should be read with that in mind.

Two more clocks are waiting: whether the animals carved on a pillar at Göbekli Tepe match the constellations of 10,950 BCE better than chance, and whether the orientations of ancient monuments pile up at any one ancient date.

## What this does not settle

- **How old the constellations are.** Not tested. It would take what the oldest sources say about the figures as wholes, not the positions of single catalogued stars.
- **Whether anyone watched the pole drift before Hipparchus,** who is credited with discovering it.
- **The limits of the work itself.** AI systems designed, ran and replicated the study. No historian of astronomy has reviewed it.
