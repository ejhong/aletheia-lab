## The idea

Bird-headed men, feathered serpents, teachers who arrive by sea and bring the arts of civilisation, a flood that ends a world, a stone that marks the navel of the earth. The same forms turn up in ancient cultures that, on the usual account, never met.

The atlas's [Deep Memory](https://ejhong.github.io/aletheia/cases/deep-memory/) case asks how much of that resemblance belongs to a shared past, and how much to the ways people everywhere make images, tell stories and look for connections. That is a question about texts and images, and the atlas weighs it.

The lab comes at it from the other side. A shared past is a physical claim. Either people travelled between the cultures, or knowledge was handed down across a very long stretch of time. Travellers leave descendants, and descendants carry their ancestry in their bones. Old astronomy leaves a date in the sky, because the stars drift in a way that can be calculated. Neither kind of trace depends on reading a myth the right way. A third test does use the stories, but only by counting where they are told: if flood myths remember the sea rising, they should be commonest where the sea took the most land.

This page has also become the lab's shelf for history and ancient civilisation in general. Further down are two tests of a lost Ice Age civilisation, by its smoke and by its crops, two of disputed writing (a letter that quotes a secret gospel, and a claim to have read the script of Minoan Crete), and a search of Inca knotted-cord records for copies.

## Did anyone cross?

Stories of lost seafarers and of contact between the Old World and the New before Columbus all make the same prediction. People who travel and settle leave descendants. Thousands of ancient people have now had their DNA read and published. If the travellers existed, some of them should be there.

- **The Australasian signal in the Amazon.** A few Amazonian peoples do carry a faint resemblance to New Guineans and Aboriginal Australians, and it is real. But it leads back to mainland Asia tens of thousands of years ago, and came into the Americas over land with the first settlers. It does not point to a voyage across the Pacific.
- **Strangers in the record.** Among nearly thirteen thousand ancient people, nobody before Columbus is buried on the wrong side of an ocean, apart from the medieval Norse of Greenland, whom history already knew. The one person the search could not explain turned out, on a check of her raw DNA, to carry modern contamination.

{{figure: strangers/map}}

Both searches found what was already known, which shows they could see. Neither found anything else.

Read more: [the Amazon signal](../../questions/population-y/) and [the search for strangers](../../questions/strangers/).

## Is the map of the sky older than history?

The Earth's axis wobbles once in about 26,000 years, and the pole of the sky moves among the stars as it does. So anything mapped from the stars, or aligned to them, carries a clock. Writers on lost civilisations have often claimed that these clocks read 10,500 BCE or thereabouts.

{{figure: topic-deep-memory/sky-plate}}

One clock has been read so far. The classical constellations leave a patch of the southern sky empty, centred on where the south pole stood for whoever set its edge. The date it gives is that of the Greek astronomers who compiled the star catalogue, between about 130 BCE and 140 CE.

That is the right answer to the question the record can answer, and the wrong question for the purpose. A cataloguer lists every star he can see, so the edge of the listed stars is his own horizon. The figures those stars belong to may be far older, and the catalogue is silent on it. The study also showed how soft such dates are: the model named in advance as the main one put the catalogue in 910 CE, centuries after it was written, because of one assumption about haze near the horizon.

Read more: [the hole in the ancient sky](../../questions/ancient-sky-hole/), where the sky can be wound back by hand.

A second clock could not be read. A 2017 paper takes the animals carved on a pillar at Göbekli Tepe for constellations and the pillar for a record of the sky in 10,950 BCE, with odds of one in 5 million against coincidence. Those odds rest on the authors' own ranking of which carving looks like which constellation. We asked three vision models to rank the same pictures blind. None could match even the scorpion carving to the scorpion constellation, which was the check set in advance for trusting them, and they disagreed with one another. So the test gives no verdict. It does show that the paper's number is a measure of its authors' judgement: with the models' ranks in the same formula, one in 5 million becomes about one in two.

Read more: [Göbekli Tepe's star map](../../questions/gobekli-star-map/), with the pictures the judges saw.

## Do flood myths remember drowned Ice Age coasts?

When the Ice Age ended the sea rose about 120 metres and covered wide plains: under the North Sea, between the islands of Southeast Asia, around Australia, between Siberia and Alaska. If the world's flood stories are a memory of that, they should be commoner among peoples whose coast lost a great deal of land than among peoples whose coast lost almost none.

In Yuri Berezkin's catalogue of the myths of 927 peoples, matched to a map of the sea floor, they are not. Among 396 peoples living near water, 36% of those beside the narrowest drowned shelves have a flood motif and 34% of those beside the widest. The model fixed in advance, which compares peoples within the same language family, finds no link.

This is a weaker "no" than it first looked. A spot check found that a tenth of the "drowned shelf" was the Caspian Sea, the Great Lakes and dry basins; that a third of the peoples counted as having a flood myth have only motifs that are not clearly about floods; and that one of the plan's two checks on the method failed. Mending what could be mended left the answer the same. A modest effect is not excluded, and the catalogue has almost nothing from Australia, where the best-known stories of particular drowned shores come from.

Read more: [flood myths and drowned land](../../questions/flood-myths/), with a map of all 927 peoples.

## Did anyone smelt or farm before the Ice Age ended?

The usual reply to "where are the ruins?" is that the sea rose 120 metres and covered them. Two things a civilisation makes are harder to drown: the smoke of its furnaces, which settles in ice and peat far away, and the pollen of its crops, which settles in lakes. Both were looked for, and both tests turned out too blunt to answer.

- **Smoke.** No stretch of ice or peat older than 5,000 years shows lead pollution at three times its usual level. But the same records show nothing of the copper smelting that archaeology dates to 7,000 years ago, and the test as fixed would not have picked up the Roman Empire. What is ruled out is a lead industry of medieval European size, near Europe, in the stretches of time sampled. For most of the Ice Age there were no northern data at all.
- **Crops.** No farming signal appears in lake mud older than 12,000 years. But the detector found only 23% of known farming-era cores in Europe, where the plan required half, and none in the Fertile Crescent in the centuries when farming began there. By the plan's own rule the result is inconclusive.

Both first reports claimed a clean "no". Spot checks withdrew that.

Read more: [smelting before the Bronze Age](../../questions/lost-metallurgy/) and [farming before the Ice Age ended](../../questions/ice-age-farming/).

## Texts and records

**The Secret Gospel of Mark.** In 1958 Morton Smith found a letter, ascribed to Clement of Alexandria, that quotes two passages from an unknown longer version of Mark. Whether it is ancient or a modern forgery has been argued ever since. A comparison of style could not say who wrote the letter: it is too short, and the same measures fail on a short work that is certainly Clement's. The firm result concerns the quoted passages. They share more two-word phrases with Mark, word for word, than any stretch of Mark shares with the rest of it. That fits a close imitation and gives no date for it.

**Linear A.** The script of Minoan Crete has never been read. A 2026 proposal reads it as a Semitic language. The test matched Linear A words to Semitic roots and found them no better than Greek words. But Hebrew words are no better than Greek on that test either, so it cannot decide, and the proposal's author had already said this kind of matching is not evidence. His main evidence was not tested.

**Khipus.** The Incas kept accounts on knotted cords. A search of the 619 that have been measured and published found pairs carrying the same numbers in the same order: copies. Five pairs were not in the literature we could search, and one, split between Paris and Lima, matches over about 174 cords. They are leads for someone who can examine the objects. The first report called them eight discoveries with no chance of error; two had been published in 2005, one was a single khipu catalogued twice, and the search missed a known copy.

Read more: [Secret Mark](../../questions/secret-mark/), [is Linear A cracked?](../../questions/linear-a/) and [the khipu search](../../questions/khipu/).

## What the studies add up to

Ten studies so far. Four found no trace where a shared past should have left one: the DNA shows no travellers, the one star clock read so far shows the date of the people known to have set it, and flood stories are no commoner where the Ice Age sea took the most land. Four could not decide, because the tool was not up to the question: the carved pillar at Göbekli Tepe, the smoke, the crops and Linear A. Two found something: the passages quoted from the Secret Gospel are an unusually close echo of Mark, and some Inca khipus are copies of others, a few of which seem not to have been noticed.

That is not the same as showing the resemblances are coincidence. Every one of these tests has blind spots, set out below and on its own page. None of them touches the stories and images themselves, and the flood test says nothing about stories of particular drowned bays and islands, such as those collected around Australia.

A pattern in the four that could not decide is worth noting. Each time, the first report read silence as a "no", and a check of what the method could have seen took that back. A test that cannot detect the thing when it is known to be there says nothing when it finds nothing.

Waiting their turn: whether the bringers of civilisation in old stories came from the sea, whether basic words are shared across the oceans, whether germs and parasites tell a different story from the bones, and one more star clock, the orientations of ancient monuments.

## What this does not settle

- **The resemblances themselves.** Whether a bird-man in one place is the same idea as a bird-man in another is the atlas's question, not the lab's.
- **Travellers who left no sampled descendants.** Ancient DNA sees only the people who happen to have been dug up and sequenced. Egypt, Mesopotamia and the Indus valley are almost empty in the public record.
- **How old the constellations are.** A test of the figures' own age would need what the oldest sources say about them as wholes, not the positions of single catalogued stars.
- **A lost civilisation's smoke and crops.** Both tests were too blunt. A sharper one would use peat near old ore fields, and seeds and tools from excavated sites, and would first show it can see the beginnings we know about.
- **Who wrote the Letter to Theodore, and what Linear A says.** Style could not settle the first, and the second was tested with the wrong tool.
- **Stories of particular drowned places.** A catalogue of motifs counts world-flood themes. It does not hold stories about a named bay or island going under, and it treats all of Australia as eight regions.
- **Knowledge passed on without people or star maps:** by word of mouth, which leaves no physical trace at all.
- **The limits of the work itself.** AI systems designed and ran every study, and replicated or spot-checked each one. The six newest (flood myths, smoke, crops, Secret Mark, Linear A, khipus) have been spot-checked, not replicated. No specialist has reviewed any of it.
