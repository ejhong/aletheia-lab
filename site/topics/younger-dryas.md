## The idea

About 12,900 years ago the warming at the end of the last ice age went into reverse. The north turned cold again and stayed so for more than a thousand years. That cold spell is the Younger Dryas.

The impact hypothesis says a comet caused it: fragments struck or burst over North America, lit fires across continents, darkened the sky, and brought down the Clovis people and the great Ice Age animals with the climate. It is one of the main supports for the larger idea of a lost world destroyed in a cataclysm.

Most scientists who study the period think the cold had an ordinary cause, a slowing of the Atlantic currents that carry heat north, perhaps helped along by volcanic eruptions. The two accounts predict different things. A strike from the sky is an instant: its marks should be sharp, should appear together, and should be unlike anything at the ice age's other cold snaps. A change in the ocean is slow, and should look like the two dozen sudden coolings that came before it.

Each study here takes one mark, fixes the test before looking at the data, and asks whether the mark is there.

## The order of events

An impact happens on one day. Greenland's ice keeps a record layer by layer, so it can say what came first. The figure puts four records from the ice on one clock.

{{figure: topic-younger-dryas/timeline}}

Read from left to right:

1. **Eruptions come first.** Four of them fall in the 150 years before the cold, two of them very large. The last is about twenty years before the cold begins.
2. **The cold arrives slowly.** At most of the ice age's sudden coolings the temperature record steps down within a few decades (the grey line). At the Younger Dryas it drifts down over two centuries.
3. **The platinum comes after the cold has started.** By two separate ways of matching the ice cores it lies 18 to 26 years after the published start, give or take about ten.
4. **The ammonium, the "smoke", comes later still** and builds over a century.

Nothing in the ice marks a single moment when everything changed at once.

## Study by study

### Smoke in Greenland's ice

Ammonium reaches the ice from fires, soils and plants. Around the start of the Younger Dryas it ran higher, for about a century, than at almost any time in 100,000 years, and three ice cores agree. That is a real anomaly, and it is what first made this look worth testing. But it begins thirty to forty years after the cold set in and climbs slowly, where fire from the sky should mark the ice at once.

The follow-up asked whether the rise is special. It is not unique: ammonium goes up after most of the ice age's sudden coolings. The Younger Dryas rise is about three times the usual size, second and third largest of 25 in two cores, with two much older coolings close behind.

### A burn across the planet

Supporters have argued that about a tenth of the world's plant life burned. Fire on that scale would change the carbon in the whole atmosphere, and Antarctic ice traps samples of that air. The shift at the Younger Dryas is small, comes late, and is matched at other cold spells. The best-dated charcoal records from lakes around the world show no peak either.

Read more: [the global burn study](../../questions/yd-global-burn/).

### The cooling itself

Part of the hypothesis's appeal is the feeling that the cold came too fast and too hard to be natural. Set beside the others, it did not. In its first sixty years the Younger Dryas cooled more slowly than 16 of 18 other sudden coolings, and its dust rose least of all 25. Its full depth, about five degrees in Greenland, is middling. Antarctica did not cool, as it would in a worldwide winter.

This does not rule out a comet that set off the ordinary ocean mechanism. It removes the argument that the cooling needs a special cause.

Read more: [the cooling-shape study](../../questions/yd-cooling-shape/).

### Platinum, and the eruptions

A layer rich in platinum in one Greenland ice core is the best physical evidence offered for an impact, since platinum is rare in the Earth's crust and commoner in meteorites. The first platinum study tested when it arrived. The answer is awkward for both sides: after the published start of the cold, by twenty to thirty years, but before the steepest part of the fall. By the rule fixed in advance that is "ambiguous".

The same study checked the critics' alternative. The 150 years before the cold hold one of the heaviest runs of volcanic fallout in 51,000 years: third of 284 such stretches in the record that matches eruptions between Greenland and Antarctica, twelfth of 338 in Greenland's own. That fits the volcanic account. It does not prove it. Across the other eighteen coolings, eruptions are no commoner beforehand than at random times, and measured only against its neighbours in time the Greenland figure is high without being extreme.

A follow-up study then tested the chemistry itself across the public geochemical record, benchmarking Younger Dryas sediments directly against confirmed Chicxulub dinosaur-extinction impact layers and real volcanic gas condensates. The Chicxulub positive control passed cleanly (89% chondritic on six-element pattern, 100% on mixing, tight platinum-to-iridium median 1.29). Younger Dryas sediments failed every cosmic test: across 27 complete six-element samples from Hall's Cave (Texas) and Page-Ladson (Florida), zero were chondritic and 93% matched continental crust or volcanic gas; platinum-to-iridium ratios scatter a hundredfold across claimed sites on three continents (3.6 to 1,264); and blind peak scans without boundary labels put 71% to 93% of chemical spikes outside the Younger Dryas boundary—including higher platinum 1,350 years later and higher iridium 420 years earlier at Hall's Cave. At the same time, Page-Ladson matches volcanic aerosols cleanly while Hall's Cave's episodic osmium drops fit neither meteorites nor high-rhenium volcanic gas condensates alone, leaving the formal verdict mixed on the terrestrial side.

Read more: [the platinum timing and volcanism study](../../questions/yd-platinum-volcanism/) and [the platinum chemistry study](../../questions/yd-platinum-fingerprint/).

### The great animals

If a strike 12,850 years ago brought down North America's Ice Age giants, large plant-eaters should have held steady until then and vanished at once afterward. Neither appears in the two public records we tested. In nine of eleven calibrated lake and cave cores across seven US states, dung-fungus spores from giant herbivores had already fallen past half their peak a millennium or more before the cold began (median 14,669 years ago), though three sites stayed high into the Younger Dryas. Direct bone radiocarbon dates do drop sharply around 12,800 to 12,600 years ago—yet modern AMS collagen dates leave ten mammoths, mastodons, a giant beaver and a short-faced bear living 250 to 1,900 years past the claimed impact, putting nearly seven times more fossil probability inside the Younger Dryas than radiocarbon-plateau smearing can produce. Extinctions were staggered across genera by more than a thousand years.

Read more: [the megafauna timing study](../../questions/yd-megafauna-timing/).

### People

If the catastrophe was real, the dated traces of people should thin out when it struck. In nearly 7,000 radiocarbon dates from North America, Europe and Asia they do not. This is the weakest of the tests. Radiocarbon dating is at its most distorted in exactly those centuries, more than the test allowed for, so the dates show no crash but cannot rule one out.

## What if the comet kept coming?

There is another form of the hypothesis with no single day in it. A giant comet broke up in the inner solar system and left a stream of debris, of which the Taurid meteors seen each autumn are the remains. In this account the Earth ran through the thick of the stream, and fragments burst in the air again and again over a long time (Napier 2010, abstract read; the longer account by Clube and Napier was not re-read).

This version is harder to test, because it predicts no single sharp mark. Set against the same records it does better than the single day in some places and no better in others.

| What the record shows | A single day | Airbursts for years |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing in the ice marks one instant | Against it | No longer against it |
| Ammonium high for a century | Against it: smoke should arrive at once | Fits the length |
| Platinum laid down over a decade or two | Awkward: fallout from one event clears in a few years | Fits better |
| The cold starts before the platinum and before the ammonium | Cannot be the trigger | Still cannot be the trigger |
| The century of ammonium is a raised steady level, with fewer fire spikes than usual | Says nothing either way | Against repeated fires |
| Antarctica did not cool in two centuries, and Greenland's dust rose least of 25 coolings | A winter of a few years would not show | Against a long veil of dust, which would show |
| The same rise in ammonium follows other coolings | Not needed to explain it | Not needed to explain it |

**The new check.** If fragments set fires year after year, the smoke should reach Greenland as it always does, in plumes that last days. In ice sampled thirty to forty times a year, that means short spikes standing above the year's ordinary level. We looked. In the 150 years after the cold began the ordinary level of ammonium is the highest of 29 such stretches between 10,400 and 15,000 years ago, 2.7 times what it had been. Spikes are scarcer than usual: 4 years with a spike of five times the level, against 18 in the 150 years before and a typical 19. The whole rise is in the steady level, which is what soils, plants and changed winds do, and not what repeated burning does. This was an exploratory look, made after the results were known, in one ice core.

**Where that leaves it.** The drawn-out version survives where the single day does not, mostly because it predicts less. Where it does predict something we could check, a long season of fires and a long veil of dust, the ice shows neither. Its one real point is the platinum: a layer laid down over ten or twenty years is easier to get from repeated input than from one event. But the platinum stops after those years, and it begins after the cold.

**What would test it properly.** The stream is said to have crossed the Earth's path many times over thousands of years. Each crossing should have left the same marks. Platinum, or the cosmic dust that a disintegrating comet sheds, measured right through the ice age and not only at this one layer, would show whether such crossings happened and whether coolings followed them. Nobody has made that measurement, and it is beyond what public data allow.

**Calibrating on the airburst we know.** Tunguska, in June 1908, fell within the dates of a Taurid shower, and a published paper says it left an ammonium spike in ice from Greenland's summit. We looked for it in the rest of Greenland, with the test fixed before the layers of 1908 were opened. Averaged over six sites, 1908 is an ordinary year. Site by site it is not: a sharp spike with soot at two of the six and at the summit, nothing in the north-west. That is how smoke arrives, and the ice cannot say whether Tunguska or that summer's forest fires made it.

Either way it bears on this version of the hypothesis. If the spike was Tunguska's, an airburst leaves a sharp spike, and a century of airbursts would fill the ice with them; the ice of that century has fewer than usual. If it was the summer's fires, Tunguska left nothing the ice can see. Read more: [the Tunguska study](../../questions/tunguska-calibration/).

**Looking for the swarm itself.** The theory says the stream still carries large fragments, herded by Jupiter onto orbits that bring them back to the Earth every few years. Two NASA archives can be asked: the catalogue of near-Earth asteroids, and thirty-two years of airbursts seen by satellite. Neither shows a swarm. The evidence first offered for one, that the largest asteroids on such orbits point the same way as the comet, is still there: 11 of 17. But those are the same seventeen bodies as in 2010, and among the hundreds of smaller ones found since, which a break-up should have made in greater numbers, the pattern is absent. Airbursts are no commoner in the Taurid weeks, or in the years the swarm was due back. There is one hint, among asteroids down to about 150 metres, of a few more on the swarm's orbits than chance gives. Read more: [the Taurid swarm study](../../questions/taurid-swarm/).

This is a test of the stream today. A swarm could have broken down in 12,900 years.

## What the studies add up to

This section is the reading of the AI models that ran these studies, after all ten. It is not the atlas's judgment, which is made [there](https://ejhong.github.io/aletheia/cases/ydih/) by its own process.

**The single day of fire.** This version made the sharpest predictions and they have failed one after another. There is no burn in the world's air or its charcoal. The ammonium in Greenland comes late and builds slowly. The cold itself began more slowly than most of the ice age's sudden coolings and went no deeper. Greenland's platinum arrives two or three decades after the cold has started, and across the multi-element geochemical record none of the full-suite Younger Dryas samples looks like a meteorite or Chicxulub impact clay, platinum-to-iridium ratios scatter a hundredfold across sites, and blind scans find most spikes at the wrong depths. North America's giant animals began declining a millennium beforehand in most lake cores and still walked the continent centuries after the claimed strike. People's traces do not thin, though that last test was a blurred one. We count this version as very unlikely.

**The comet that kept coming.** This version dodges the objections about timing, because it predicts no single moment. It has now been tested where it does make predictions, three times, and each went against it.

- Years of fires should fill the ice with short spikes of smoke. The century in question has fewer than usual.
- The one airburst we know, in 1908, left just such a spike where it left anything, or else it left nothing that can be seen. Neither helps.
- The swarm of large fragments that is supposed to do the striking does not show in today's sky.

It can still be rescued, by a swarm that has since dispersed and airbursts that lit few fires. But each rescue removes a prediction, and a version with none left cannot be tested. We count it as unlikely, and less well defined than when we started.

**What is left standing.**

- *A handful of localized platinum and osmium anomalies without one agreed terrestrial source.* Greenland's GISP2 ice layer (with extreme Pt/Ir = 100–1,264 matching submarine volcanic gas condensates rather than meteorites), South Carolina's White Pond Pt/Pd spike, and Hall's Cave's episodic low-rhenium osmium drops across 13.3–10.9 ka remain real measurements. None passes a cosmic/Chicxulub fingerprint test, and Greenland's layer still arrives after cooling began—yet a single volcanic recipe does not neatly explain every site either.
- *Seventeen aligned asteroids.* A real pattern among the largest bodies on Taurid-like orbits. It is the same evidence as in 2010, no stronger and no weaker.
- *The size of the ammonium rise.* Among the two or three largest of 25, even though the kind of rise is common.

**What points the other way.** The ordinary account, a slowing of the Atlantic's circulation, needs nothing unusual, and the Younger Dryas looks like the ice age's other coolings in shape, in depth and in what followed. The heavy run of eruptions in the 150 years before it is the one unusual thing found in the critics' favour, and Page-Ladson's osmium-and-HSE chemistry lines up with volcanic aerosols—though neither proves volcanism caused every site's signal or the cooling itself.

**In one sentence.** After ten tests, a cosmic impact as the *cause* of the Younger Dryas or of North America's megafauna extinction looks unlikely in either form; the platinum and HSE chemistry fails every cosmic/Chicxulub fingerprint test even while leaving loose ends on the terrestrial volcanic side; and nothing here touches the claims we have not tested.

## What this does not settle

- **Full six-element ice-core and lake-core chemistry.** Greenland's GISP2 platinum layer has never been measured continuously across earlier ice-age coolings or paired with osmium isotopes and all six siderophile elements in the same ice, and White Pond's sharp Pt/Pd spike lacks iridium and osmium isotopes. Hall's Cave's recurring low-rhenium osmium drops also need a better terrestrial geochemical model than high-rhenium Kudryavy fumarole gas.
- **The traces in sediment** claimed for an impact: tiny spherules, nanodiamonds, a dark layer at many sites. None is tested here.
- **What drove the megafauna decline.** The timing rules out a single day of extinction at 12,850 years ago, but does not separate human hunting from Ice Age warming and cooling as the cause of the drawn-out loss.
- **People.** The one test made was blurred by the dating method. A better method exists and has not been tried.
- **The limits of the work itself.** AI models designed, ran and wrote up every study, mostly in single sessions. The earlier eight have been reproduced across companies' models; the two newest await replication. No human specialist has reviewed any of it.
