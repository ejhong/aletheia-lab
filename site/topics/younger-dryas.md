## Where the hypothesis stands

This is the assessment of the AI models that ran and checked the fourteen studies on this page. No human specialist has reviewed it. It is not the atlas's judgment, which is made [there](https://ejhong.github.io/aletheia/cases/ydih/) by its own process. If we have misstated anyone's position or misread a dataset, we want to know: [open an issue](https://github.com/ejhong/aletheia-lab/issues).

The hypothesis makes two kinds of claim, and on the public evidence they have come apart.

**That something fell is open.** Whether fragments of a comet struck or burst over the Earth about 12,800 years ago and left a thin layer of debris is not settled by anything here. The evidence offered is microscopic grains in the layer (tiny spheres, melted glass, nanodiamonds, shocked quartz) and platinum in Greenland's ice and in sediments. The platinum is a real measurement with no agreed source. The grains are disputed between laboratories, and no public dataset can test them. Two papers from 2025 that offered new grain evidence were retracted in 2026. Checked against their own data, the dating of one does not hold and neither shows that its layer is unlike the sediment around it; but nobody has shown anything wrong with the sixteen quartz grains in the other.

**That it caused a catastrophe looks very unlikely.** The fires across continents, the darkened sky and a comet-made cold are not in the records where they should be. That is the firmest thing these studies say.

So the question worth pursuing is the narrower one: whether an event left that layer, whatever it did or did not do to the climate. That question can be answered, and the next section says how.

Taking the claims of the version its authors hold now ([set out below](#three-versions-of-the-idea)) one at a time:

**The other two versions.** The version first proposed in 2007 fares the same on fire, climate and platinum, and worse on the animals, since it had them die at once. We also built a more generous version than anyone proposes, in which fragments kept arriving for years, to see whether stretching the event rescues the claims about fire and climate. It does not: see [the steelman](#what-if-the-comet-kept-coming-a-steelman).

**What points the other way.** The ordinary account needs no comet. The Younger Dryas matches the ice age's other sudden coolings in its shape, its depth, the opposite course of Antarctica and the ammonium that follows. The 150 years before it hold one of the heaviest runs of eruptions in 51,000 years. Neither fact shows that the ocean or the volcanoes did it alone.

**How far to trust this.** AI models designed, ran and wrote up every study, mostly in single sessions, under Eugene's direction. The first eight were each rewritten from the plan by a second company's model, which got the same answers. The six newest were run by Google's model and then spot-checked by Anthropic's, which reran their programs and corrected the write-ups; every one of the six had claimed more than its numbers showed. A spot check is not a replication. That is a reason for some caution about the first eight as well.

**In one sentence.** The fires, the darkened sky and the comet-made cold are not in the records where they should be; whether a swarm of fragments nevertheless burst over the Earth 12,800 years ago and left a thin layer of debris is a question about microscopic grains that only laboratories can answer.

## What is left to investigate

The open half of the hypothesis can be tested. In rough order of how much each test would settle:

1. **A blind count of the grains.** Samples from the layer and from above and below it, split and sent unlabelled to several laboratories, including ones that doubt the hypothesis, with the rules for counting fixed beforehand. This is the test that would show whether the layer holds something the sediment around it does not. It needs laboratories and the people who hold the samples. The sixteen quartz grains with glass-filled fractures in the retracted 2025 paper are a place to start: nobody has disputed the microscopy, and the samples above and below were never counted in the same way.
2. **The full chemistry of Greenland's platinum layer.** Platinum has been measured through the layer in one ice core. Osmium and the other platinum-group elements in the same ice would separate a meteorite from a volcano, and the same measurement at the ice age's other coolings would show whether the layer is unusual. Neither has been done.
3. **Helium-3 in polar ice across the onset.** Comet dust carries helium-3. Ice could resolve decades where sea-floor mud resolves a thousand years. The public ice record has no samples between 4,000 and 75,000 years ago.
4. **The measurements with no agreed source.** A sharp spike of platinum against palladium at White Pond in South Carolina, and repeated osmium excursions at Hall's Cave in Texas, two of them at the boundary itself. Only two sites of this age have all six platinum-group elements measured through a long section. More would show whether there is a pattern.
5. **Published grain counts, read without knowing where the boundary is.** Many papers give counts of spheres and other grains by depth. Whether the peaks pick out the boundary when its position is hidden can be tested from the literature. We have not run this.
6. **The steep fall in dated bones as the cold begins.** The timing does not separate hunting, warming, the cold, or a blow from the sky, and a list of dated bones is not a count of animals.
7. **Human numbers.** The one test here was blurred by the distortion in radiocarbon dating. A better method exists and has not been tried.
8. **Whether encounters with the comet's debris came round again.** Seventeen large asteroids on Taurid-like orbits line up with the comet. A test for a repeating mark over the last 15,000 years is proposed (#104) and not run.
9. **The size of the ammonium rise.** It is the second and third largest of 25 coolings in the two records, and follows the cold. Nothing here explains why it is so large.

## The idea

About 12,900 years ago the warming at the end of the last ice age went into reverse. The north turned cold again and stayed so for more than a thousand years. That cold spell is the Younger Dryas.

The impact hypothesis says a comet caused it: fragments struck or burst over North America, lit fires across continents, darkened the sky, and brought down the Clovis people and the great Ice Age animals with the climate. It is one of the main supports for the larger idea of a lost world destroyed in a cataclysm.

Most scientists who study the period think the cold had an ordinary cause, a slowing of the Atlantic currents that carry heat north, perhaps helped along by volcanic eruptions. The two accounts predict different things. A strike from the sky is an instant: its marks should be sharp, should appear together, and should be unlike anything at the ice age's other cold snaps. A change in the ocean is slow, and should look like the two dozen sudden coolings that came before it.

Each study here takes one mark, fixes the test before looking at the data, and asks whether the mark is there.

## Three versions of the idea

The hypothesis has changed since it was first put forward, and the studies here do not all bear on the same version. Three are worth keeping apart.

1. **As first proposed (2007).** "One or more large, low-density" objects exploded over northern North America, shook the ice sheet, and set off the cooling; the blast, the fires and the hunger that followed helped finish the great animals and the Clovis people (Firestone and others 2007, as quoted by Sweatman and others 2024, section 3).
2. **As its authors hold it now (since 2018).** A giant comet, 100 kilometres or more across, broke up in the inner solar system. About 12,800 years ago the Earth ran into the debris, and many fragments burst in the air or struck the ground across at least four continents. The fires burned about a tenth of the planet's plant life; the soot blocked the sun; meltwater from broken ice dams changed the ocean's currents and brought on the cold (Wolbach and others 2018, as quoted in the same place). The encounter is "envisaged to have occurred within the span of a single day across an entire hemisphere" (Sweatman 2021, section 2.6); the paper it rests on works with the energy of 2,000 to 10,000 Tunguska-sized bodies arriving in about an hour (Napier 2010). Two things are different from 2007. The extinctions are no longer said to be sudden: they "may play out over centuries to millennia". And the authors hold that radiocarbon dates which look centuries apart can come from one day, so scattered dates do not count against them (Sweatman and others 2024, section 4).
3. **A drawn-out version, which is ours.** Some of the marks in the ice last far longer than a day: the ammonium stays high for a century, the platinum for two decades. So we also tested the most generous reading we could build, in which fragments of the same comet kept arriving for years. No paper by the hypothesis's authors that we have read proposes this. Their wider theory, called coherent catastrophism, says only that a comet breaking up makes other encounters in the last 20,000 to 30,000 years more likely. The drawn-out version is a steelman: a stronger opponent than the one in the literature, built to see whether anything of the idea survives.

The second version is the one to judge the hypothesis by. We read its sources in the authors' accepted manuscripts on 24 November 2026; Napier's paper in full, the two by Sweatman in the sections cited.

## The order of events

An impact happens on one day. Greenland's ice keeps a record layer by layer, so it can say what came first. The figure puts four records from the ice on one clock.

{{figure: topic-younger-dryas/timeline}}

Read from left to right:

1. **Eruptions come first.** Four of them fall in the 150 years before the cold, two of them very large. The last is about twenty years before the cold begins.
2. **The cold arrives slowly.** At most of the ice age's sudden coolings the temperature record steps down within a few decades (the grey line). At the Younger Dryas it drifts down over two centuries.
3. **The platinum comes after the cold has started.** By two separate ways of matching the ice cores it lies 18 to 26 years after the published start, give or take about ten.
4. **The ammonium, the "smoke", comes later still** and builds over a century.

Nothing in the ice marks a single moment when everything changed at once.

## Study by study

### Smoke in Greenland's ice

Ammonium reaches the ice from fires, soils and plants. Around the start of the Younger Dryas it ran higher, for about a century, than at almost any time in 100,000 years, and three ice cores agree. That is a real anomaly, and it is what first made this look worth testing. But it begins thirty to forty years after the cold set in and climbs slowly, where fire from the sky should mark the ice at once.

The follow-up asked whether the rise is special. It is not unique: ammonium goes up after most of the ice age's sudden coolings. The Younger Dryas rise is about three times the usual size, second and third largest of 25 in two cores, with two much older coolings close behind.

### A burn across the planet

Supporters have argued that about a tenth of the world's plant life burned. Fire on that scale would change the carbon in the whole atmosphere, and Antarctic ice traps samples of that air. The shift at the Younger Dryas is small, comes late, and is matched at other cold spells. The best-dated charcoal records from lakes around the world show no peak either.

Read more: [the global burn study](../../questions/yd-global-burn/).

### The cooling itself

Part of the hypothesis's appeal is the feeling that the cold came too fast and too hard to be natural. Set beside the others, it did not. In its first sixty years the Younger Dryas cooled more slowly than 16 of 18 other sudden coolings, and its dust rose least of all 25. Its full depth, about five degrees in Greenland, is middling. Antarctica did not cool, as it would in a worldwide winter.

This does not rule out a comet that set off the ordinary ocean mechanism. It removes the argument that the cooling needs a special cause.

Read more: [the cooling-shape study](../../questions/yd-cooling-shape/).

### Platinum, and the eruptions

A layer rich in platinum in one Greenland ice core is the best physical evidence offered for an impact, since platinum is rare in the Earth's crust and commoner in meteorites. The first platinum study tested when it arrived. The answer is awkward for both sides: after the published start of the cold, by twenty to thirty years, but before the steepest part of the fall. By the rule fixed in advance that is "ambiguous".

The same study checked the critics' alternative. The 150 years before the cold hold one of the heaviest runs of volcanic fallout in 51,000 years: third of 284 such stretches in the record that matches eruptions between Greenland and Antarctica, twelfth of 338 in Greenland's own. That fits the volcanic account. It does not prove it. Across the other eighteen coolings, eruptions are no commoner beforehand than at random times, and measured only against its neighbours in time the Greenland figure is high without being extreme.

A follow-up study tested the chemistry. A meteorite brings platinum together with five related elements in fixed proportions, and the layer left by the asteroid that ended the dinosaurs shows them; volcanic gas and ordinary rock carry the six in different proportions. Only two sites of Younger Dryas age have all six measured through a long section: Hall's Cave in Texas and Page-Ladson in Florida. Of 27 samples there, 23 look like ordinary crust or like the site's own rhenium-rich mud, and 4 lean towards the impact pattern, two of them from the boundary itself. At Hall's Cave the highest platinum comes 1,350 years after the cold began and the highest iridium 420 years before it. So whatever raised these elements did so more than once, and not chiefly at the boundary. That counts against one great fall of cosmic material. It does not exclude a small one: at Hall's Cave a meteoritic mixture fits most samples slightly better than the volcanic gas it was compared with. The verdict by the rule fixed in advance is "mixed". A spot check found that four of the nine samples used to check the method were not samples at all; the study was rerun with the five real ones and the verdict did not change.

Read more: [the platinum timing and volcanism study](../../questions/yd-platinum-volcanism/) and [the platinum chemistry study](../../questions/yd-platinum-fingerprint/).

### The great animals

The 2007 version had the great animals die with the strike. Two public records say when they went. Fungus spores that grow on the dung of large plant-eaters, counted in eleven lake and cave cores, were already past half their peak before the cold began in nine of them, and more than a thousand years before in six. Radiocarbon dates on the animals' own bones tell a different part of the story: they are at their most numerous just before the cold, fall about tenfold as it begins, and continue as a thin tail. Ten well-dated mammoths, mastodons, a giant beaver and a short-faced bear lived 200 to 1,900 years after the claimed strike, more than the blur in radiocarbon dating can account for.

A sudden extinction of everything is ruled out. The hypothesis's authors no longer claim one: they now say the losses may have played out over centuries to millennia, with small groups surviving. The steep fall in dated bones as the cold begins is what a blow at that moment would look like. It is also what hunting or the cold alone would look like, and a list of dated bones is not a count of animals. This record cannot choose.

Read more: [the megafauna timing study](../../questions/yd-megafauna-timing/).

### Are the claimed impact layers the same age?

The hypothesis says that thin layers at 23 to 32 sites on four continents were laid down by one event. If so, radiocarbon dates from those layers should agree. At nine sites with usable dates they do not: the precise ones differ by more than their errors allow, whichever site is left out. The same method, tried on a real one-year event of the same period, the Laacher See eruption in Germany, finds its dates in close agreement.

That is weaker than it sounds, for two reasons a spot check brought out. The eruption is dated on trees it killed, while the claimed layers are dated mostly on charcoal lying in or near them, which can be older than the layer; with the dates most favourable to the hypothesis, a few decades of that would be enough to explain the disagreement. And four of the nine sites turned out to be dated on Clovis camps, not on the layer. The hypothesis's authors also hold that radiocarbon cannot be read this finely, and that scatter of this kind does not count against them.

The part that does not depend on any of this is a simple count. Of the 32 claimed sites, four have a date on the layer itself that is good to 300 years. Three-quarters of the sites the authors modelled would "match" an event 250 years earlier or later just as well. If the dates cannot show the layers differ in age, they cannot show the layers are the same age either. The verdict is "mixed".

Read more: [the site-synchrony study](../../questions/ydb-site-synchrony/).

### Two retracted papers

In February 2026 the journal *PLOS One* retracted two papers from 2025 that offered new evidence that something fell. One reported comet dust, tiny spheres and platinum-rich particles in sea-floor cores from Baffin Bay. The other reported shocked quartz at three sites in the United States. Their authors objected and called the journal's reasons procedural. Most of the data is still public, so the reasons could be checked against it.

For the Baffin Bay cores they hold. The ages printed in the paper are not the ones its stated method gives: for three of the four cores they match that method with the correction for old sea water entered with the opposite sign, which makes the layers look older. Done as stated, the four layers come out 300 to 610 years younger, and with the larger correction recommended for polar seas they may be younger than the whole cold spell. We can say which calculation reproduces the printed ages. We cannot say how it came about. The pictured "spherule fragments" are calcium carbonate, one of them plainly a microfossil, and the paper's chemical argument for comet material gives the same answer for ordinary crust.

For the shocked quartz the journal's two reasons mostly hold. The claim that three sites share one date rests on a test that could hardly have failed. Too few samples outside the layer were examined, none of them with a count of the grains searched, to show that such grains are special to it. But sixteen grains with glass-filled fractures were found where the authors said, and neither the journal nor this study examined them.

The spot check changed this study more than any other here. Its first write-up accused the quartz paper's authors of choosing a convenient date, which they had not done, and rested one result on a list of dates that did not match its sources. Both are corrected.

Read more: [the study of the two retracted papers](../../questions/yd-retracted-plos-audit/).

### People

If the catastrophe was real, the dated traces of people should thin out when it struck. In nearly 7,000 radiocarbon dates from North America, Europe and Asia they do not. This is the weakest of the tests. Radiocarbon dating is at its most distorted in exactly those centuries, more than the test allowed for, so the dates show no crash but cannot rule one out.

## What if the comet kept coming? A steelman

The hypothesis's authors describe one day. But two marks in the ice last far longer than a day, and one day cannot make them. So we also tested the most generous reading we could build: that fragments of the same comet kept arriving, bursting in the air again and again for years. Nobody we have read proposes this. We tested it because if even this version fails, there is less left to rescue.

This version is harder to test, because it predicts no single sharp mark. Set against the same records it does better than the single day in some places and no better in others.

| What the record shows | A single day (the authors' versions) | Airbursts for years (our steelman) |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing in the ice marks one instant | Against it | No longer against it |
| Ammonium high for a century | Against it: smoke should arrive at once | Fits the length |
| Platinum laid down over a decade or two | Awkward: the authors put it down to fine dust settling slowly | Fits better |
| The cold starts before the platinum and before the ammonium | Cannot be the trigger | Still cannot be the trigger |
| The century of ammonium is a raised steady level in two cores, with no large increase in big plumes | Says nothing either way | Against a large increase in fires; a modest one is not ruled out |
| Antarctica did not cool in two centuries, and Greenland's dust rose least of 25 coolings | A winter of a few years would not show | Against a long veil of dust, which would show |
| No rise in helium-3 dust in ocean mud over thousands of years | Says nothing: one day of large fragments would not show | Against a long-lived cloud of comet dust |
| The same rise in ammonium follows other coolings | Not needed to explain it | Not needed to explain it |

**Fire spikes in two ice cores.** If fragments set fires year after year, smoke should reach Greenland in plumes that last days, standing above the year's ordinary level. An exploratory look at one core (NGRIP) suggested the opposite: the whole rise sat in the ordinary level, and sharp spikes became scarcer. The test was then fixed in advance and run on a second core, NEEM, about 350 km away. The first part held. The ordinary level is 2.6 times higher after the cold began, the highest of 28 stretches, and that accounts for the whole rise. The second part did not survive a spot check. Spikes had been counted relative to a running background, and the background itself rose about fourfold, which makes spikes look scarcer by arithmetic alone. Counted in absolute terms, years with a large plume number 18 after the cold began and 14 before. That rules out a tripling of big fires and leaves room for a modest rise. The test is also blunt: at this depth a plume of a few days is smeared across most of a year of ice. Read more: [the NEEM fire-spike study](../../questions/yd-neem-spikes/).

**Looking for the comet's dust.** A comet breaking up over thousands of years should surround the Earth with fine dust for a long time. That dust carries helium-3, which fires and volcanoes cannot supply, and deep-sea mud keeps a record of it: after an asteroid broke up 8.2 million years ago the mud holds two to four times the usual amount for more than a million years. In nine ocean cores from the Pacific and Atlantic there is no rise between 20,000 and 10,000 years ago; the amount is the usual one or slightly less. That is good evidence against a dust cloud lasting thousands of years. It is weaker for anything shorter: only one of the nine cores can tell one thousand years from the next, and that core rules out a rise of more than about a third. A single day is beyond the method altogether, and helium-3 rides only on fine dust, not on large fragments. One of the three checks that the method works also failed, so by the rule fixed in advance the verdict is "mixed". Read more: [the cosmic-dust study](../../questions/yd-cosmic-dust-he3/).

**Where that leaves it.** The drawn-out version survives where the single day does not, mostly because it predicts less. Where it predicts something we could check, the checks went against it or found nothing: no large increase in fire plumes in two Greenland cores, no long veil of dust, no long-lived cloud of comet dust in ocean mud. Its one real point is the platinum: a layer laid down over ten or twenty years is easier to get from repeated input than from one event. But the platinum stops after those years and begins after the cold. On current public data this version looks **unlikely**. Since nobody proposes it, its failure costs the hypothesis little. What it shows is that stretching the event over years does not rescue the claims about fire and climate.

**Calibrating on the airburst we know.** Tunguska, in June 1908, fell within the dates of a Taurid shower, and a published paper says it left an ammonium spike in ice from Greenland's summit. We looked for it in the rest of Greenland, with the test fixed before the layers of 1908 were opened. Averaged over six sites, 1908 is an ordinary year. Site by site it is not: a sharp spike with soot at two of the six and at the summit, nothing in the north-west. That is how smoke arrives, and the ice cannot say whether Tunguska or that summer's forest fires made it.

Either way it bears on this version of the hypothesis. If the spike was Tunguska's, an airburst leaves a sharp spike, and a century of airbursts would fill the ice with them; two Greenland cores show a raised background and no large increase in big plumes. If it was the summer's fires, Tunguska left nothing the ice can see. Read more: [the Tunguska study](../../questions/tunguska-calibration/).

**Looking for the swarm itself.** The theory says the stream still carries large fragments, herded by Jupiter onto orbits that bring them back to the Earth every few years. Two NASA archives can be asked: the catalogue of near-Earth asteroids, and thirty-two years of airbursts seen by satellite. Neither shows a swarm. The evidence first offered for one, that the largest asteroids on such orbits point the same way as the comet, is still there: 11 of 17. But those are the same seventeen bodies as in 2010, and among the hundreds of smaller ones found since, which a break-up should have made in greater numbers, the pattern is absent. Airbursts are no commoner in the Taurid weeks, or in the years the swarm was due back. There is one hint, among asteroids down to about 150 metres, of a few more on the swarm's orbits than chance gives. Read more: [the Taurid swarm study](../../questions/taurid-swarm/).

This is a test of the stream today. A swarm could have broken down in 12,900 years, though if it had broken down into fine dust that lasted thousands of years, the ocean cores would probably show it.
