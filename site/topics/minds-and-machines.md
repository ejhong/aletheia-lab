## The idea

Some of the boldest claims about minds are arguments, not experiments. Roger Penrose argued from Gödel's incompleteness theorem that human mathematical insight cannot be the work of any computer. The "unfolding argument" runs the other way round: it says that any system with feedback loops can be rewritten as one without them that behaves identically, so theories that tie consciousness to such loops are either false or beyond testing.

Arguments like these have been disputed for decades, partly because nobody agrees on what exactly they assume. A proof checker does not take sides. Written out formally, an argument either goes through from its stated premises or it does not, and the checker shows which premise carries the weight.

## What the studies add up to

- **Gödel and the mind.** Checked by machine. Penrose's modest conclusion is proved. His proof of the stronger one, that the mind is no machine at all, does not hold up. The stronger conclusion itself is neither proved nor refuted, and nothing in the check touches his physics.
- **The unfolding argument.** Checked by machine. A system with feedback loops does have a loop-free copy that behaves the same, but only for an experiment of fixed length, and the copy needs one more part for every further step. The swap the other way, bolting an idle loop onto a system, lasts forever. So the argument reaches a theory on which loops are enough for consciousness however long one watches, and a theory on which loops are only needed for experiments of fixed length. In both forms it rests on one assumption: that behaviour is the only evidence there is about consciousness. A further check found that when the parts of a system may remember their own state, whether its loops can be removed for good depends on how big a part may be: memory bits without feedback can count to eight forever and can never count to three.

Two further questions wait their turn: a standing watch on whether machines come to do what Penrose said they could not, and a test of the physical side of his theory, that anaesthetics act on the microtubules inside nerve cells.

## What this does not settle

A proof checker can say what follows from what. It cannot say whether the premises are true of real minds. An argument that fails as a proof may still have a true conclusion.
