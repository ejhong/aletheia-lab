# Was there an exceptional fire 12,800 years ago? Results

Issue #43 · Lane: data · Protocol locked 2026-11-23T07:50:14Z · No amendments

## Answer

**Verdict by the rule set in advance: "unusual". There is a real ammonium high in all three Greenland cores
close to the start of the Younger Dryas. It is among the largest of the last 100,000 years when measured over
decades. It is not a sharp spike, it is not the largest, and it does not sit at the onset: it begins 30 to 40
years after the cold set in and lasts about a century.**

The window tested is 300 years around the onset (12,750–13,050 years before 2000 CE). The measure is the largest
excess of ammonium over its local background. Rank 1 is the highest of all windows.

## What the feature is

### A century-long high that starts after the onset

In NGRIP the onset is fixed at 12,896 ± 4 years before 2000 (Rasmussen et al. 2014). It marks an abrupt shift;
the cooling and the dust build up over the following century (see "How the main explanations fit"). Ammonium in
10-year means, µg/kg:

| Years before 2000 | NGRIP | NEEM |
|---|---|---|
| 12,900–13,010 (the century before the onset) | 7–10, one decade at 29 | 4–19 |
| 12,870–12,900 (the first 30 years of the cold) | 12–15 | 17–19 |
| 12,750–12,870 (the high) | 17–52 | 14–58 |
| 12,700–12,750 | 17–41 | 14–31 |

The decades around the onset have some of the lowest ammonium of the stretch. The rise begins near 12,860, some
30 to 40 years after the onset, and peaks between 12,760 and 12,830, which is 70 to 140 years after it. In GISP2, on its own timescale, the
high runs from about 12,735 to 12,840 years BP.

With windows of 120 years, tight on the onset (12,840–12,960), nothing stands out anywhere: rank 95 of 807 in
NGRIP at 10 years, 83 of 328 at 20 years, 60 of 326 in GISP2, and in the bottom half at 60 years in all three
cores.

### Broad, not sharp

Exploratory, from annual NGRIP bins: the median year has 7.0 µg/kg in the 94 years before the onset and 25.5
during the high; 47 of 108 years in the high exceed 30 µg/kg, against 4 of 91 before the onset. The single
largest annual excess in the window (113 µg/kg) is at 13,043, before the onset, and ranks 10th of 31.

This is why the ranks improve as the bins lengthen. A single great fire season would do the opposite.

### High in absolute terms

Peak height (H), reported but not part of the rule because ammonium rises and falls with climate:

| | NGRIP | NEEM | GISP2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20-year bins | 44.8 µg/kg, **rank 1 of 131** | 47.7, rank 2 of 131 | 34.9, rank 2 of 132 |
| 60-year bins | 37.7 µg/kg, **rank 1 of 323** | 36.7, rank 3 of 322 | 30.6, rank 2 of 323 |
| 10-year bins | 52.4, rank 11 of 323 | 58.3, rank 10 of 319 | |

The 60 years from 12,750 to 12,810 hold the highest ammonium concentration of any 60-year bin in NGRIP between
10,300 and 107,620 years ago. With the Holocene included, GISP2 still ranks it second (of 365 windows at 60
years, of 165 at 20 years).

Two things lift it apart from smoke. Ammonium was already high through the late-glacial centuries (the local
background here is 17–29 µg/kg, against a median of about 6 for the whole record). And snow was scarcer after
the onset: NGRIP's annual layers are 3.0 cm thick after it and 4.0 cm before (exploratory), so the same
deposition gives about a third more concentration.

The score that compares a peak with the local scatter (Z) is ordinary everywhere: rank 102 of 131 in NGRIP at 20
years, 254 of 323 at 10 years.

## The checks set in advance

**Other widths.** At 600 years (12,600–13,200) the ranks are about the same: 3 of 65 (NGRIP, 20 years), 4 of 64
(NEEM), 2 of 65 (GISP2); 8, 4 and 3 of about 160 at 60 years. In 3-year bins NGRIP reaches rank 2 of 81, on a
peak at 12,693, some 200 years after the onset. At 120 years: nothing, as above.

**Grid moved by half a bin.** 20 years: ranks 3, 5, 2. 60 years: 8, 5, 3. GISP2 at 60 years would then reach
the 1% line; no second core would.

**One-sided backgrounds.** With the background taken as the higher of the medians before and after a bin, the
60-year tier gives ranks 1 (NGRIP), 3 (NEEM) and 4 (GISP2), with the NGRIP and NEEM peaks in the same bin. **That
variant would have passed at 1%**, which is the rule's "exceptional". With the lower of the two medians: 1, 4
and 4, which would not. The 20-year tier is unchanged by either (4, 3, 3 and 4, 2, 3). The verdict stays
"unusual": the rule uses the centred background. But the result sits close to the line, and which side of it
depends on how the background is drawn.

**Nitrate.** NGRIP: excess rank 5 of 320, peak at 12,830. NEEM: rank 198 of 320. GISP2 at 60 years: rank 12 of
323. Nitrate does not show the same thing in the three cores.

**The cited GISP2 samples.** Between 1711.5 and 1713.0 m ammonium runs from 5.6 to 49.6 ppb; the sample at
1712.2–1712.3 m, the depth supporters are reported to cite, has 31.4 ppb. The largest sample in the window is
higher up, at 1708.8–1708.9 m (12,771–12,775 years BP on the file's scale): 77.3 ppb, which exceeds 99.93% of
the 11,038 dated samples. Seven samples in the file are higher; six are Holocene and one is at 1756.0 m, about
13,886 years BP. This comparison is not fair to old ice, where samples are longer.

**Do the cores move together?** Yes: the rank correlation of window excess between NGRIP and NEEM is 0.57
across 318 windows.

## What the test could see

One-year pulses of made-up ammonium were added at 300 random places in each series. Share whose window then
reached the series' top 1% (and top 5%):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | 1% (9%) | 0% (4%) | 0% (3%) | 0% (7%) | 1% (6%) | 2% (7%) | 1% (6%) | 0% (9%) |
| 500 | 92% (100%) | 1% (97%) | 26% (99%) | 1% (99%) | 21% (92%) | 10% (82%) | 2% (29%) | 1% (69%) |
| 1,000 | 100% | 99% | 100% | 98% | 95% | 99% | 74% (99%) | 63% (94%) |
| 2,000 | 100% | 99% | 100% | 99% | 97% | 100% | 100% | 93% |

For scale: ordinary ammonium is about 6 µg/kg, so a pulse of 1,000 is some 170 years' worth in one year. The
rule would catch a single-year event of that size almost always, and would miss one of 200 or less. So "no sharp
spike" means no spike of several hundred units, not no fire.

The feature found adds up to about the same amount, spread out: its excess times the bin length is roughly 300
to 800 µg/kg·yr across the tiers.

## What this does and does not show

**Shown.**

- Supporters are right that Greenland ice holds an unusual ammonium high near the start of the Younger Dryas. It
  appears in three cores, two laboratories and two measuring methods, at matching times in the two cores that
  share a timescale.
- Over 20 to 60 years it ranks third to seventh among 131 to 323 windows. In raw concentration it is first to
  third.
- It is not a spike at the onset. Around the onset itself ammonium is low. The high begins decades later and
  lasts about a century.
- It is not alone. Late-glacial ice has several highs of similar size, one of them larger in two cores.

**Not shown.**

- What caused it. Ammonium in Greenland ice comes from fires and from soils and plants (Fischer et al. 2015). A
  century of raised ammonium could be more burning, for instance of forest dying in the cold, a change in
  sources or winds, or thinner snow concentrating the same fallout. This test cannot tell these apart.
- That an impact did or did not happen. A fire set off by an impact at the onset should show at the onset;
  ammonium there is low. A century of raised burning that follows an abrupt cooling does not need an impact and
  does not rule one out.
- Anything about fires outside the reach of Greenland's air.

## How the main explanations fit

Added on 23 November 2026, after the result, at Eugene's request. Everything here is exploratory
(`explore/volcanic.py`) or is reasoning about the result; none of it was in the locked plan.

### What marks the onset, and what follows it

The onset at 12,896 years before 2000 is defined mainly from deuterium excess, a sign of where the moisture came
from, which "is shown to change between states in 1–3 years"; "the shifts in δ18O and [Ca2+] are more gradual
and begin no more than a couple of decades after" (Rasmussen et al. 2014, read). So the onset is an abrupt shift,
and the cooling and the dust build up after it.

Ammonium goes with the gradual part. In 20-year means (µg/kg; NGRIP and NEEM in years before 2000, GISP2 on its
own scale):

| | Century before | The shift and the next ~45 years | 45 to 145 years after | Later in the Younger Dryas |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NGRIP ammonium | 8–19 | 11–28 | 24–45 | 17–42 |
| NGRIP dust (calcium) | 27–44 | 34–51 | 68–174 | 153–315 |
| NEEM ammonium | 5–11 | 14–18 | 26–48 | 14–27 |
| NEEM dust | 27–38 | 31–53 | 70–120 | 138–220 |
| GISP2 ammonium | 7–12 | 14–20 | 25–35 | 14–21 |
| GISP2 dust | 23–36 | 38–55 | 60–166 | 131–195 |

Columns for NGRIP and NEEM: 12,910–13,010; 12,850–12,910; 12,750–12,850; 12,510–12,750. For GISP2, the matching
stretches on its own scale, 15 years younger.

In all three cores ammonium climbs while the dust climbs, over about a century. Ammonium then falls back part of
the way; dust stays high.

### A single impact that lit fires

Smoke reaches Greenland within days and falls out within weeks. Fires lit at the onset should put an ammonium
spike at the onset. Ammonium there is low, and the largest single year in the window is ordinary. **Poor fit**,
on timing and on shape.

### Airbursts, with no crater

The form of the hypothesis in which the object broke up in the air changes how the fires were lit, or, in one
proposal, makes ammonia directly: Melott et al. 2010 (*Geology* 38:355–358; abstract read on arXiv:0907.1067)
suggest that "direct input of ammonia from a comet into the atmosphere is adequate for YD ice core data", made by
"an analog of the Haber process". Either way the ammonium would arrive promptly. Material spread through the
whole atmosphere does not take decades to come down: the large volcanic eruptions in this same ice, which do
load the whole atmosphere, appear as sulphate spikes a single sample wide (four to five years).

So an airburst at the onset fits **no better and no worse than an impact**: the ice does not show a prompt
ammonium pulse of several hundred units (see "What the test could see"). A smaller prompt pulse could be there
and unseen. Nitrate, which the same paper expects from the shock of an airburst, is high in NGRIP (rank 5 of
320), ordinary in NEEM (198 of 320) and in between in GISP2, so the cores do not agree on it.

A long series of airbursts over a century cannot be excluded by ammonium. Nothing in the ice asks for it either:
the rise follows the dust, as a response to the changing climate would.

### Volcanism

Critics have proposed that an eruption, or several, set off the cooling. An eruption makes sulphate, not
ammonium, so this account predicts no ammonium at the trigger and leaves any ammonium change to be a response of
land and air to the cold: delayed, gradual, moving with the other climate signs. That is what the ice shows.
**Good fit for the ammonium**, though the same is true of any trigger that is not itself a source of ammonium.

GISP2 records sulphate in the same samples, so the order of events there needs no timescale:

| Depth (m) | Age on the file's scale | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1718.6 | 13,031 | Sulphate spike, 649 ppb against a background of 58; the third largest between 10,300 and 15,000 |
| 1713.8 | 12,930 | Sulphate spike, 462 ppb against 60; the sixth largest |
| about 1712 | about 12,880 | Dust begins to rise |
| 1710.7 to 1708 | 12,840 to 12,740 | Ammonium high; dust still rising |
| 1704.4 | 12,605 | The largest sulphate spike of the stretch, 986 ppb, well inside the Younger Dryas |

So two large eruptions precede the dust rise by about 50 and 150 years. That is in the right order for a
volcanic trigger, and it is weak support: spikes as large as the smaller one come about once in 800 years here,
so one falling within 50 years before any given moment has roughly a 6% chance. By the window test the sulphate
around the onset is not unusual (rank 36 of 132 at 20 years, 211 of 323 at 60).

The eruption most often named is Laacher See. Reinig et al. 2021 (*Nature* 595:66–69) date it to 13,006 ± 9 cal
BP and the Younger Dryas onset to 12,807 ± 12 cal BP, about 200 years apart, and argue against a direct link;
this is from a search summary of the abstract, `unverified`, and the date has been disputed. We have not
identified any sulphate spike with any eruption.

### A change in ocean circulation

The standard account is that fresh water slowed the Atlantic circulation (from memory; not sourced here). For
ammonium it predicts the same as volcanism: nothing at the trigger, a response afterwards. **Good fit**, and no
test here separates it from volcanism.

### Summary

| Explanation | What it predicts for ammonium | Fit with the ice |
|---|---|---|
| One impact that lit fires at the onset | A sharp spike at the onset | Poor: low at the onset; a slow rise later |
| Airburst at the onset (fires, or ammonia made in the blast) | A prompt pulse, within a few years | Poor, unless the pulse was small |
| Airbursts spread over a century | Raised ammonium for a century | Not excluded; not called for |
| Volcanic trigger | None at the trigger; a response to the cold | Good; two large eruptions shortly before, which is weak support |
| Ocean circulation | None at the trigger; a response to the cold | Good; untested here |

The ammonium high looks like part of the climate's shift into the cold state. It fits explanations in which
ammonium answers the change better than explanations in which ammonium is the smoke of whatever caused it.

- **Sources not read.** Wolbach et al. 2018 is closed-access and the hosts with copies refused automated

  and ammonium peaks reported for GISP2 are unverified.
- The plan's reason for setting peak height aside ("warm periods have higher ammonium") does not fit this case:
  the high is in a cold period. Peak height is reported in full above.
- Everything under "exploratory" (`explore/after.py`, `explore/volcanic.py`) was done after seeing the result.

## Limits

- **Concentration, not deposition.** Not corrected for snowfall, which fell at the onset.
- **Ranks, not probabilities.** Windows are not independent draws; late-glacial windows cluster at the top.
- **One comparison set.** The window was not compared with other abrupt coolings only.
- **GISP2's timescale** is not tied to the others here, and its anchor depth is unverified. The window was wide
  enough that this did not matter for the ranks.
- **The background rule matters** at 60 years, where it rests on five bins. See the one-sided variant.
- **NGRIP and NEEM share a laboratory and method.** GISP2 does not.
- **One session** proposed, designed, ran and wrote up the question.

## What this means

Ice from Greenland keeps a year-by-year record of ammonium, a chemical that northern wildfires, soils and plants
put into the air. People who think a comet struck the Earth 12,900 years ago point to a burst of ammonium in
that ice as the smoke of the fires it lit. We asked a plain question with the rules fixed first: does that
stretch of ice stand out from every other stretch? It does, partly. For about a hundred years ammonium ran
higher than at almost any other time in a hundred thousand years, and three separate ice cores agree. But the
high does not begin when the cold begins. Ammonium is low at that moment; the rise comes thirty to forty years later
and builds over decades, where a continent set alight in a day should mark the ice at once. And the same
centuries hold other highs as large. So the ice does record something unusual near the start of the Younger
Dryas, and the people who said so were right about that. What it records looks more like a century of change in
northern forests and air than like one catastrophic fire, and nothing in this test says what set it off.
