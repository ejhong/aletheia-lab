# Does ammonium rise after every abrupt cooling? Results

Issue #60 · Lane: data · Protocol locked 2026-11-23T14:58:15Z · No amendments

## Answer

**A rise in ammonium after an abrupt cooling is common, not universal. The Younger Dryas rise is one of the
largest of 25, not the largest, and two other coolings come close to it in both ice cores. The Younger Dryas is
the top of a range, not outside it.**

By the rules fixed in advance:

| Question | Verdict | The numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Is a delayed rise general among the other coolings? | **Mixed** | A rise after 15 of 24 in NGRIP and 17 of 24 in NEEM. "General" needed 16 in both. |
| Where does the Younger Dryas rank? | **Among the largest** | 2nd of 25 in NGRIP, 3rd of 25 in NEEM. |

The measure, D, compares ammonium 40 to 160 years after a cooling begins with the century before it. As a
multiple:

| | NGRIP | NEEM |
|---|---|---|
| The Younger Dryas (GS-1) | ×3.2 | ×3.5 |
| The largest other cooling | ×3.9 (GS-18, 63,840 years ago) | ×4.2 (GS-15.1, 54,900 years ago) |
| The median other cooling | ×1.2 | ×1.7 |
| Other coolings with any rise at all | 19 of 24 | 17 of 24 |

Two independent programs, written by OpenAI's and Google's models from the protocol alone, reproduce every
value, both verdicts and both ranks.

## What it means for the Younger Dryas

Question #43 found a century of high ammonium beginning a few decades after the Younger Dryas cold set in, and
asked whether that was unusual. This test says:

- **Some rise is the normal thing.** After most abrupt coolings, ammonium in the ice goes up, typically by a
  fifth to three-quarters. So part of the Younger Dryas high is what the ice does after any cooling.
- **The Younger Dryas rise is about three times the typical one**, and near the top of the 25.
- **It is not alone at the top.** The cooling that began 63,840 years ago (GS-18) rose by ×3.9 in NGRIP and ×2.3
  in NEEM. The one that began 74,100 years ago (GS-20) rose by ×2.6 in both. Nobody has proposed an impact for
  either.
- **The two cores often disagree.** Only 10 coolings show a rise in both; 12 show it in one and not the other;
  2 in neither. The Younger Dryas is the only cooling above ×3 in both.

So the Younger Dryas high is an extreme case of a common pattern. It is weaker evidence for something unusual
than it looked in #43, and it is not nothing.

### In proportion and in amount

D is a ratio. In micrograms per kilogram of ice the Younger Dryas is far ahead, because ammonium levels at the
end of the ice age were several times higher than in the depth of it:

| | Century before | 40 to 160 years after |
|---|---|---|
| Younger Dryas, NGRIP | 10.6 | 33.8 |
| GS-18, NGRIP | 2.2 | 8.7 |
| GS-20, NGRIP | 2.9 | 7.7 |

Which comparison is the fair one depends on what raises ammonium. If the sources multiply, ratios compare like
with like. If a fixed amount is added, the Younger Dryas added four times more than any other cooling. This
study cannot say which.

### The other measures

| Measure | General among the others? | Younger Dryas rank (NGRIP, NEEM) |
|---|---|---|
| D, the delayed century (decides) | mixed: 15 and 17 of 24 | 2 and 3 of 25 |
| P, the largest 20-year mean in 200 years | mixed: 10 and 17 of 24 | 2 and 5 |
| R, the mean of all 200 years | general: 17 and 17 of 24 | 2 and 4 |

The fall-back measure, H, asks whether the delayed century stands above the level 200 to 300 years after the
onset. For the Younger Dryas it does (×1.3 in NGRIP, ×1.8 in NEEM; 4th and 5th of the 21 coolings long enough to
measure). For the others the median is ×1.0: their rise is a step that stays.

### Controls

| Chemical | Rises after the other coolings (NGRIP, NEEM) | Typical rise | Younger Dryas rank |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium (dust) | 24 of 24, 23 of 24 | ×2.7, ×2.4 | 13 and 7 of 25 |
| Sodium (sea salt) | 23 of 23, 21 of 22 | ×1.5, ×1.6 | 10 of 24, 1 of 23 |
| Nitrate | 20 of 24, 20 of 24 | ×1.2, ×1.2 | 5 and 11 of 25 |
| **Ammonium** | **15 of 24, 17 of 24** | **×1.2, ×1.7** | **2 and 3 of 25** |

Dust behaves as it should, so the dates and windows catch the transitions. By dust the Younger Dryas is an
ordinary cooling. Everything measured rises in concentration after a cooling; less snow falls in the cold, and
the same fallout in less snow reads higher. Ammonium's typical rise is about the size of nitrate's and sodium's,
so much of the "common rise" may be that effect and not more ammonium in the air.

Exploratory: with nitrate's rise subtracted from ammonium's, the Younger Dryas is 3rd of 25 in NGRIP (behind
GS-18 and GS-20) and 2nd in NEEM (behind GS-15.1). Its standing does not change.

### How much the dates matter

Every onset but the Younger Dryas moved, and the test rerun:

| Onsets moved | Rise shown (NGRIP, NEEM) | Verdict | Younger Dryas rank |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 years younger | 7 and 12 of 24 | mixed | 1 and 1 |
| 20 years younger | 9 and 15 of 24 | mixed | 2 and 1 |
| as published | 15 and 17 of 24 | mixed | 2 and 3 |
| 20 years older | 15 and 19 of 24 | mixed | 2 and 2 |
| 40 years older | 18 of 24 and 18 of 23 | general | 1 and 3 |

The count of rises swings with the dating; the standing of the Younger Dryas does not move far. The pattern
(fewer rises when the window is pushed later) says that at the other coolings the rise comes at once and fades
somewhat, which the shape below also shows.

Against only the quiet times within 10,000 years of each cooling: 14 and 17 of 24; still mixed.

### The shape (exploratory)

Each cooling's 20-year means, divided by its own level in the century before; the median across the 24 others
beside the Younger Dryas (`explore/after.json`):

| Years after the onset | Other coolings, NGRIP | Younger Dryas, NGRIP | Other coolings, NEEM | Younger Dryas, NEEM |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–20 | ×1.4 | ×1.3 | ×1.4 | ×1.9 |
| 20–60 | ×1.1–1.3 | ×1.8–2.2 | ×1.7–1.9 | ×1.8–2.3 |
| 60–160 | ×1.2–1.4 | ×2.9–4.2 | ×1.4–1.6 | ×2.4–4.5 |
| 160–300 | ×1.1–1.3 | ×1.6–3.9 | ×1.1–1.7 | ×1.4–2.5 |

The others step up when the cold begins and hold. The Younger Dryas makes the same step and then climbs again,
60 to 160 years in. That second climb is what sets it apart.

One caution about the Younger Dryas's own figure. The century before its onset was itself a low: ammonium had
been about twice as high 150 to 300 years earlier. Measured against the 200 years before, the Younger Dryas is
2nd of 18 in NGRIP and 6th of 16 in NEEM (exploratory).

### Coolings left out of the test

For the reader, with no rule applied. Two short cold spells inside the warm period before the Younger Dryas
show rises of ×1.7 and ×1.4 (GI-1d, 14,075 years ago) and ×1.6 and ×1.4 (GI-1b, 13,311). GS-14 (49,600), left
out because its onset is dated only to within a century, shows ×2.6 and ×1.8. The 11.4 ka event shows nothing.

## Deviations and mistakes

- No amendment. The program is unchanged since the lock.
- The first plan measured the wrong thing: the largest single 20-year mean, which is a peak and not a
  century-long rise. Both reviewers said so and the measure was rebuilt before the lock (`review/README.md`).
- The ordering of the table by two-core average, the shape, the nitrate comparison and the longer baseline were
  all made after the result and are exploratory.
- The event table was transcribed by a script from the paper's text, and the uncertainty classes by eye. The
  dust control passing at 47 of 48 is some assurance that the dates are right; it is not a proof-read.

## Limits

- **The Younger Dryas is not a fresh case.** The measure was shaped on it, so its own value is flattered. The
  fair reading is of the other 24, and of how close they come.
- **Concentration, not fallout.** Not corrected for snowfall.
- **The settings differ.** The Younger Dryas came as the ice age was ending, with far more vegetation in the
  north than at any other cooling here. Its larger amount could be that and nothing else. There is no second
  cooling in a like setting to compare it with.
- **Dating.** The other onsets are placed to within 20 to 60 years, and the count of rises moves with that.
- **Two cores from one laboratory**, which disagree with each other on half the coolings.
- **25 events.** Second and third of 25 is a description, not a probability.
- **One session** proposed, designed, ran and wrote up the question, knowing #43's results.

## A lead, not a finding

GS-20, the cooling that began 74,100 years ago and shows the third-largest rise in both cores, is near in time
to the Toba eruption, one of the largest known. That is from memory and `unverified`; we have not checked the
dating or the literature. If it holds, the largest ammonium rises after coolings may keep company with large
eruptions, which would bear on the volcanic explanation of the Younger Dryas. It would need its own question.

## What this means

Greenland's ice shows that the last ice age was broken by about two dozen sudden turns to cold. After the most
famous of them, the Younger Dryas, the ice holds a century of unusually high ammonium, a chemical that fires,
soils and plants put into the air, and some take that as the smoke of a catastrophe. We asked whether the other
sudden coolings left the same mark. Mostly they did, in a smaller way: ammonium usually goes up after a cooling,
by a fifth to three-quarters. The Younger Dryas rise is about three times that, and near the top of the list,
but two much older coolings come close to it and nobody thinks a comet caused those. So the high after the
Younger Dryas is the strongest case of something the ice does again and again. It remains a little odd, in that
it climbs a second time after the usual step. It is no longer a mark that only one event has left.
