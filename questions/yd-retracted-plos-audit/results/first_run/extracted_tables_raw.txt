Foraminifera: Radiocarbon Dating and Environmental Setting 
 
Radiocarbon Dating 
Sediment samples from cores 83023-52, 2013029-64, 2008029-67 and 2013029-77 were soaked in glass 
beakers overnight in reversed osmosis or nanopure water. Disaggregated samples were washed over a 63 
micron stainless steel sieve using reversed osmosis or nanopure water and the mud fraction was retained 
in a large beaker or evaporating basin for sedimentological analysis. The sand fraction retained in the 63 
micron sieve was dried in an oven at 40 oC. Sieves were washed with a methylene blue solution between 
samples to stain any particles, including foraminiferal tests, retained in the sieve. This process precludes 
contamination between samples. 
The dried sand of each sample was split using an Otto microsplitter and an aliquot was spread on a 45-
square black-painted, brass picking tray. Using a random numbers table to select grid squares, 
approximately 200 specimens of foraminifera per sample, where possible, were picked and transferred 
to microslides for assemblage analysis. In samples where foraminifera were rare, all specimens were 
picked. The remaining sediment was picked of all remaining hyaline calcite benthic foraminifera or of 
an amount to provide enough material for radiocarbon dating (SI Table S17). The resulting hyaline calcite 
mixed benthic foraminifera picks were composed of ca. 1000 to ca. 4700 specimens (cores 52 and 77). 
Foraminiferal assemblages comprising porcelaneous benthic foraminifera (high magnesium calcite tests; 
core 67) were excluded from the specimens picked for radiocarbon analysis. Where assemblages were 
dominated by planktic foraminifera (core 67) only a few hundred specimens were required to attain 
enough calcite for radiocarbon analysis. Samples in core 64 were composed of hyaline calcite mixed 
benthic and (less abundant) planktic foraminiferal tests. The tiny, delicate species, Stetsonia arctica, 
provided little calcite, and thus contributed little to samples for radiocarbon dating even when it was a 
dominant species in assemblages.

supports cosmic airbursts/impacts contributing to North American 
megafaunal extinctions and collapse of the Clovis technocomplex 
 
James P. Kennett, Malcolm A. LeCompte, Christopher R. Moore, Gunther Kletetschka,

This document provides supplementary data, figures, and methodological details to support the 
findings presented in the main manuscript. 
The supporting information is organized into three main sections: 
1. Supporting Tables include detailed age calibrations for the Younger Dryas Boundary 
(YDB) layer at the three study sites and comprehensive data on YDB proxies found at each 
location. 
2. Additional Figures: This section presents high-resolution images and analytical data from 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), energy 
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), and other advanced techniques. These figures provide 
visual evidence supporting the identification of shock- fractured quartz and other impact -
related materials. 
3. Additional Methods: This section offers detailed descriptions of the analytical techniques 
employed in this study, including sample preparation methods and specific protocols for 
each type of analysis. 
This information is intended to assist with a better  understanding of the data collection and 
analyses and present additional evidence supporting the conclusions drawn in the main manuscript. 
 
Supporting Tables 
Table S1. Comparison of age calibration by IntCal13 and IntCal20. The latest calibration 
curve, IntCal20, differs from the previous one, IntCal13. Using both curves to calibrate the same 
radiocarbon date (10,900 ± 50 14C y) results in an average difference of +40 y. Consequently, the 
YDB age range that was calculated by Kennett et al. [1] is revised from 12,835-12,735 cal BP to 
12,875-12,775 cal BP.
