# Does Tunguska show in the ice? Results

Issue #100 · Lane: data · Protocol locked 2026-11-23T20:12:12Z · No amendments

## Answer

**Across Greenland as a whole, no. Taken over six sites away from Summit, the ammonium of 1908 and 1909 ranks
10th of 56 pairs of years: "ordinary" by the rule fixed in advance. But 1908 is not empty. Two of the six sites
hold a large spike, the Summit spike that prompted the question is confirmed in two more cores, and two sites in
the north-west hold nothing. The mark is real, strong where it falls and absent elsewhere, which is how smoke
arrives. The ice cannot say whether Tunguska or the fires of that summer made it.**

| Chemical | Sites | Rank of 1908–09 | Verdict | Sites in their own top tenth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ammonium** | 6 | **10 of 56** | **Ordinary** | NEGIS |
| Nitrate | 5 | 10 of 56 | Ordinary | NEEM, NGRIP |
| Black carbon | 3 | 30 of 92 | Ordinary | none |
| Sulphur (a check) | 5 | 23 of 56 | Ordinary | none |

The method sees a known event: the window of the Katmai eruption, 1912–13, ranks 2nd of 56 for sulphur, behind
1884–85 (Krakatau).

An independent program written by Google's model from the protocol alone reproduces every rank, score, size and
verdict.

## How large a mark could have been missed

With the real data in place, a pulse added to every site in one year was called elevated or better in 89% of
trials when it was two typical years' ammonium, and in 100% when it was four. A pulse of one typical year was
caught in 32%. If only half the sites received it, a pulse of four was caught in 52%.

So a mark of two typical years' worth or more *at every site* would very probably have shown. What 1908 left is
a mark far larger than that at some sites and none at others, and a test built on the average of sites calls
that ordinary. The verdict is about Greenland as a whole. It is not a statement that nothing happened.

## What the spikes look like (exploratory)

Looked at after the result, sample by sample (`explore/after.py`). Not part of the locked test.

- **They are sharp.** At NEGIS the largest sample is 32 times a typical one and the spike spans about half of
  1908. In the two Summit cores the largest samples are 50 and 15 times typical and the spike is a few samples
  wide.
- **They come with soot at Summit.** Black carbon within the spike is 2.7 and 4.0 times typical in the two
  cores, and the window ranks 6th for black carbon in each. Nitrate is about double.
- **They line up on mid to late 1908.** Dates inside a year are only good to a season. A steadier clock is the
  gap to each core's own sulphur peak from Katmai, which erupted in June 1912. The ammonium spike lies 4.2 years
  before that peak in both Summit cores, 4.3 at NGRIP, 4.5 in one Tunu core and 3.9 at ACT11d. If Katmai's
  sulphur peaked some months after the eruption, that puts all of them in the second half of 1908, including the
  one that ACT11d's own dates place in 1909. Tunguska was on 30 June 1908. The northern fire season is July to
  September.
- **The mark that prompted the question is real.** The GISP2 sample of 1908 to 1910 that we saw first is the
  same event, and it belongs to 1908, not to the great fires of 1910.
- **A second event shows nearby.** Two Tunu cores set aside for doubtful dating hold a very large spike dated
  1906 (23 and 39 times a typical sample) that the two better-dated Tunu cores do not show, and NEGIS and one
  Summit core have smaller spikes in 1906. We have not resolved whether those are one event or two, or how four
  cores at one site can differ so much.

## What this says about the Younger Dryas

An illustration of scale, as the protocol sets out, and no more.

In the 150 years after the Younger Dryas cold began, the ammonium reaching NGRIP each year was about 2.3 times
what it had been: about 1.3 typical earlier years' worth extra, every year.

- **In yearly totals.** The verdict is ordinary, so no rate is worked out from the size. If Tunguska left a mark
  across Greenland it was probably less than two typical years' worth, and matching the rise would then take
  more than about two such events every three years, for a century and a half.
- **In shape, which matters more.** Whatever made the 1908 mark, where it shows it is a spike of weeks to
  months, tens of times the usual level, with soot, strong at some sites and missing at others. A century of
  such events would be a forest of spikes. The century after the Younger Dryas began has fewer spikes than
  usual and a raised steady level (#43).

That leaves two readings, and neither helps the idea that Tunguska-like airbursts made the Younger Dryas
ammonium. If the 1908 spike was Tunguska's, an airburst leaves a kind of mark that is scarce in the Younger Dryas
ice. If it was the summer's fires, then Tunguska itself left nothing these records can see.

Limits of that comparison: Tunguska was in Siberia, far from Greenland and not upwind of it, and one event says
little about many; the air, the snowfall and the thickness of the layers in the Younger Dryas were different; a
different kind of object, or far smaller and far more numerous ones, need not behave like Tunguska.

## Deviations and mistakes

- No amendment. One line of the program was changed after the lock, to write numpy numbers to the results file;
  the first run had stopped there before printing anything. No computation changed.
- **The first plan was rebuilt after review.** It used overlapping windows, moved three cores' dates by a rule
  the reviewer showed to be unsafe, and turned a null result into a bound it could not support
  (`review/README.md`).
- **The window width, thresholds and choice of cores were fixed after seeing the reference years**, though not
  1905 to 1912.
- **The test as designed answers a narrower question than the one that matters.** It asks about a Greenland-wide
  mean. The finding of interest is the pattern across sites, which the plan reports but does not test.
- Papers named by the reviewer on the fire origin of such spikes (Wolff et al. 2012; Duderstadt et al. 2014,
  2016; Legrand et al. 2016) were not read.

## Limits

- **An airburst cannot be told from a fire year.** Smoke raises ammonium and nitrate together. Soot at Summit
  points to smoke, but Tunguska burned forest too.
- **Four of the six test sites share a laboratory and a family of chronologies.**
- **About 56 reference windows**, from the 1850s to the 1960s.
- **Dating.** A core a year out would move the mark out of the window. ACT11d's high year is 1909 by its own
  dates and 1908 by its distance from Katmai.
- **Yearly means blur short events.** The sample-by-sample look is exploratory.
- **The standard tables are our reading of thirteen differently laid-out files.**
- **One session** proposed, designed, ran and wrote up the question, knowing the Summit observation.

## What this means

In June 1908 something exploded in the sky over Siberia and flattened a forest. If such explosions are what
filled Greenland's ice with ammonium at the start of the Younger Dryas, the one we know about should have left a
sign. We looked in the ice of 1908 at six places in Greenland, with the test fixed before looking. Averaged over
all six, 1908 is an ordinary year. Place by place it is not: there is a sharp, sooty spike at two of the six and
at Greenland's summit, and nothing in the north-west. That is the patchy mark smoke leaves, and it could be from
Tunguska or from that summer's forest fires; the ice cannot tell. For the Younger Dryas the lesson is the same
either way. An event like 1908 writes a spike into the ice. The century after the Younger Dryas began has fewer
spikes than usual and a slow, steady rise, which is not what a rain of Tunguskas would leave.
